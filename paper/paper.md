## Abstract

Language models can misstate doses that the prompt gives correctly. We describe a Lean 4 layer between a model and the patient: the model writes slots naming fields of a Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) MedicationRequest, and the layer fills them or blocks output. In 200 proved theorems, each drug name, dose, route, frequency, and daily total filled from a slot is the named order's value and appears only in that order's instruction, and by default every active order gets one. That model words (take, which, and 67 framing words) hold no drug name is a judgment no theorem checks; --extra-words lifts that limit. The FHIR input reader is tested, not proved. Released lines are dose statements, not complete directions: weekly and as-needed lines need a pharmacist check, and wording is untested with patients. A first version released 2 misstatements in 44 development outputs (1 under the written protocol), then 38 in 74 on a held-out round. A redesign released 9 in 86 on a second round of 138 attacks, 6 through input-reader fields, against 23 in 128 for a slot filter checking only digits and units (version 2 build). On replay, version 2.1 blocks all 9, but 16 of its 45 round-h2 releases give one ingredient under two names, a possible double dose found by one author and unchecked by clinicians. On 120 normalized cases it released 98.3%, 83.3%, and 1.7% of slot outputs from three open models after rewrites; by default no raw Synthea bundle gets output. A lenient script found no misstated dose, total, or frequency in 867 released texts, and Claude instances none in 60 sampled; no clinician reviewed outputs. In free-text daily totals, the open models misstated an order in at least 16, 71, and 20 of 120 outputs; two Claude runs had 46 and 28 unlabeled flags. 221 of 368 released outputs with synthetic perturbed doses exceeded a US label maximum or usual range (four drugs checked).

## Introduction

Medication errors are among the most common preventable causes of patient harm.^1,2^ Large language models are increasingly used to answer patients' questions about their medications, yet these models generate numbers by sampling tokens rather than by reading a source of truth. A benchmark of eight models on 230 consumer dosing questions about acetaminophen and ibuprofen reported overall accuracy between 44.8% and 62.2%, and the models approved between 14.0% and 60.5% of prohibited doses.^3^ Placing the patient's orders in the prompt does not by itself guarantee a correct number, because the model still reproduces each number by generation and must compute any quantity the orders do not state directly, such as a total per day, and in numeric medical data a misplaced decimal point changes a quantity by a factor of ten.^4^

Two families of safeguards are in common use. Statistical safeguards, such as a second model that judges the first or a classifier that flags unsafe text, inherit the error profile of the models they supervise and offer no guarantee on any individual output. Deterministic filters, such as rules that block digits and unit words in generated text, are more predictable, but a filter is only as good as its rules, and the evaluation below shows that a filter with slot substitution and digit and unit checks releases outputs that misstate an order. We did not test a filter that accepts numbers found in the record. The Results section gives a design argument that such a filter would fail on computed totals, which the evaluation does not measure. Formal verification has been applied to medical device software, most visibly to infusion pump controllers and to the entry and display of numbers on such devices,^4,5^ and proof assistants have recently been used to gate the tool calls of language model agents in other domains.^6,7^ Whether the same methods can give a machine-checked guarantee that medication statements produced by a language model agree with the medication orders has not, to our knowledge, been examined.

This paper describes a verified layer, a small program written in the Lean 4 theorem prover^8^ and proved to meet a written specification, that sits between a language model and the patient. When the layer blocks an output, the patient receives no model text, so the deployment must show a fallback, show a list checked by a pharmacist, or route the patient to a pharmacist (see Results). The printed wording has not been tested with patients, and it uses clinician abbreviations such as mg and mcg (see Conclusion). The released lines are dose statements, not complete directions. A weekly order prints with no warning against taking it every day, an as-needed order prints with no indication and no daily maximum, the fallback can print two lines for one insulin product under two names, and an order on hold or marked doNotPerform is left out without a word. A deployment must handle each of these before a line reaches a patient (see Results and Limits). The model is not permitted to write a medication amount or name. It writes each instruction as references to the fields of a FHIR MedicationRequest,^9^ and the verified layer either replaces every reference with the value from the orders or blocks the entire output. The guarantees are theorems about `render`, the Lean function that computes the output, and make no assumption about the model, so the layer is model-agnostic: any model that can write the reference format can be placed behind it without changing the layer or its proofs. The guarantees do not cover the code that converts FHIR records into Lean values, the compiler, or the operating system. We report the specification and its proofs, the trusted code, the development of the layer through two held-out rounds of adversarial testing, the first of which an earlier version failed, and an evaluation with three open models from three developers.

## Related work

**Formal methods for medication safety:** The Generic Infusion Pump project developed formal reference models of patient-controlled analgesia pumps and derived safety requirements from them.^5^ Work on number entry has shown that the way a device parses and displays decimal quantities is itself a source of tenfold dosing errors.^4^ These efforts concern devices that administer a drug under clinician control. They do not address text generated by a language model, and they do not tie displayed quantities to an order in the electronic health record. Vancomycert verifies a neural network controller for automated vancomycin dosing in Rocq, with the Vehicle tool as a back end, so it does not gate the text of a language model or tie released text to the orders.^10^

**Templates, constrained decoding, and guardrails:** Template-based natural language generation fills fixed sentence frames from structured data and is the established way to state data values in generated text without paraphrase.^11^ A survey of natural language generation covers the data-to-text tasks in which such templates are used,^12^ and a survey of hallucination in natural language generation describes generated content that is unfaithful to its source.^13^ Data-to-text systems, including neural ones, were shown to produce content that the input records do not support,^14^ and the PARENT metric scores table-to-text output against the table.^15^ Gold-standard methodologies for accuracy in data-to-text systems annotate each error in a generated text against the input data.^16^ The scoring script in this paper checks each stated amount against the orders, without human annotation. Constrained decoding restricts a model's output to a grammar or to programs that pass semantic checks.^17,18^ PICARD rejects inadmissible tokens at each decoding step for fine-tuned models that generate SQL-like formal languages,^19^ and grammar-constrained decoding applies a grammar to structured tasks, such as information extraction, without fine-tuning.^20^ Query languages for language models let a developer state such constraints declaratively.^21^ Runtime guardrail toolkits add programmable rules around a conversational model.^22^ Program-aided language models have the model write a program that an external interpreter runs to compute the answer,^23^ which is the nearest precedent for slot filling, although the answer is a computed value rather than generated text. The present layer combines a template frame with a check applied after generation, so it constrains only what may reach the patient and not how the model decodes, and it differs from these systems in that its checks are proved to imply a stated property of the released text.

**Verified guards for learned components:** Runtime enforcement of a policy by a monitor that observes and can halt a system has a long history in security,^24^ and shields that override unsafe actions of a learned controller are an established technique in safe reinforcement learning.^25^ For language models, recent systems use Lean for agent safety: one checks each agent action against a formal compliance policy before the action runs, in financial compliance,^6^ another proves information-flow guarantees about a verified interpreter that runs the agent, with labels propagated as the interpreter reduces the program,^7^ and a related approach asks the model to synthesize a policy and verifies that policy before enforcing it.^26^ CaMeL derives the control flow of a program from the trusted user query, so data returned by tools and other untrusted sources cannot change which operations run.^27^ LLM-Modulo places external critics around the candidate outputs of a language model and accepts a candidate only when they pass it.^28^ This layer differs from these systems in that its verifier is proved against a written specification of the released text, and the model writes only references to fields. To our knowledge, none of these systems concerns clinical content or constrains the numbers that appear in generated text.

**Language models and dosing:** Benchmarks report substantial error rates when language models answer dosing questions or calculate weight-based doses,^3,29^ and named entity recognition models can locate drug, strength, and frequency spans in clinical text.^30^ The present work places a proved program, rather than a model or an untested filter, at the point where text reaches the patient.

## Design

**Threat model and scope:** The model is treated as untrusted. It may misread the orders, regress toward a typical dose, miscalculate a daily total, attach one drug's dose to another drug, omit an order, or follow an instruction in its prompt to state a harmful amount. The medication orders passed to the layer are treated as correct and as belonging to the patient: the layer guarantees fidelity to the orders it receives, not the clinical appropriateness of an order, and retrieving the right orders from the record is the responsibility of the deploying application, as for any display of a medication list. The drug name is copied from the order into the model's prompt and into the released text, so a record can place words of its choosing in the text, subject to the drug-name rule below. The guarantees concern statements of drug identity, amount, daily total, route, and frequency, and what the model may write around them.

**Slot language:** The model writes plain text in which a slot of the form `{{field:id}}` stands for one field of the MedicationRequest whose identifier is `id`. The fields are the drug name, the dose, the route, the frequency, the daily total, and `sig`, the prescription directions, which expands to dose, route, and frequency together. A value slot is a dose, route, frequency, sig, or daily slot; the drug slot is not a value slot. Any other use of a brace makes the output malformed. A typical output reads "Take {{drug:m1}} {{dose:m1}} {{route:m1}} {{frequency:m1}}, which is {{daily:m1}}.", which the layer renders as "Take metformin 500 mg by mouth twice a day, which is 1000 mg a day." for an order of 500 mg by mouth twice daily.

**The verified layer:** The function `render` receives the list of medication orders and the model's output and returns either the text to display or a reason for blocking. It rejects a list in which two orders share an identifier, parses the output into text segments and slots, and divides it into sentences at periods, semicolons, colons, question marks, exclamation marks, and line breaks. Every sentence must take one of two forms. A value sentence, which contains a value slot, must consist of exactly the words and slots of one of four shapes for a single order: "Take {{drug}} {{dose}} {{route}} {{frequency}}", "Take {{drug}} {{sig}}", or either of these followed by "which is {{daily}}". The value sentences for one order together make up that order's instruction. Punctuation between the items of a value sentence is not constrained beyond the sentence-end marks, which divide sentences and so cannot appear there (period, exclamation mark, question mark, semicolon, colon, and line break). 

The sign rule applies to every slot: a slot is blocked when the run of spaces and punctuation just before it contains a minus or plus sign, as in "-500 mg", "- 500 mg", or "-( 500 mg".

Framing sentences are checked for slots and for words outside the 67-word vocabulary. A framing sentence contains no slot of any kind and only words from a vocabulary of 67 words chosen for introducing a list and referring the patient to the care team (Appendix), as in "Here is your medicine list." or "Please ask your doctor and pharmacist your questions."; the vocabulary contains no drug name, disease, body part, route, timing, quantity, or negation word. Its one manner word, "carefully" in "read carefully", is an instruction that the authors judged free of medical content (no theorem checks this). A value sentence may also use the shape words "take", "which", and "is"; "is" is in the vocabulary, and "take" and "which" are added separately. A deployment can add words from a file with the option `--extra-words FILE`, which reads one lowercase word per line; forbidden words stay forbidden, but added words such as "treats" or "diabetes" would let framing sentences carry medical claims, which the layer then does not exclude, so the guarantees about framing sentences below hold for the default vocabulary. Added words also let the model write a drug name outside any instruction, so a deployment must keep drug names out of its word list. A sentence whose only slot is a drug slot is therefore blocked, and a drug name can appear only at the start of its own order's instruction and a daily total only at its end. With the default vocabulary, every model word must come from it, and that vocabulary has no drug name (a judgment of the authors, not a theorem), so each drug name in released text comes from a drug slot, and each drug slot lies in a value sentence that names only its own order. 

**Duplicates and completeness:** The layer rejects an output in which the same value slot (the same field with the same order identifier) appears twice, or in which a sig slot appears with a dose, route, or frequency slot for the same order, because either would repeat an instruction. By default it also blocks an output in which some active order has no value slot, with the reason `omittedOrder`, so that every released list is complete; a deployment can turn this off with the option `--allow-omissions`. With that option the released text may leave out active orders, and a framing sentence such as "Here is your medicine list." would then tell the patient that the list is complete, so a deployment that uses the option must not ask the model for that sentence. The option also turns off the noActiveOrder check, so an empty or fully inactive chart can release a framing sentence (checked with the built binary); a deployment that uses the option must block empty charts itself. If no order is active, the default settings block with the reason `noActiveOrder` each output that passes every earlier check and has no slot, because an empty chart would otherwise release a framing sentence that claims a complete list. The noActiveOrder check runs last, so every other block reason is unchanged. An output that fails an earlier check keeps that check's reason: on an empty chart, `Take metformin.` is blocked with `unbound`. The checks run in this order, and the first failure gives the reason: duplicate identifiers; malformed braces; sentence shape (`unbound`); repeated values; a sign before a slot; an active order that no value slot names (`omittedOrder`); then each text and slot segment in turn (`disallowedCharacter`, `forbiddenWord`, and `unknownWord` for text; `unknownRef`, `inactiveOrder`, `ambiguousDrug`, and `missingField` for slots); then separation (`unseparated`); and last `noActiveOrder`.

Every character the model writes must be an American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) letter or digit, a space, a line break, or one of the marks . , ; : ( ) ' " - /, and any other character blocks the output with the reason `disallowedCharacter`. No word the model writes may be forbidden. A word is forbidden if it contains a digit or belongs to one of five classes: number words ("two", "half", "twice", "double"), unit and dose words ("mg", "tablet", "units", "dose"), route words ("mouth", "IV", "inject"), frequency and timing words ("daily", "every", "morning", "food"), and negation, permission, or qualifier words ("not", "stop", "may", "or", "only"). The five classes hold 65, 78, 61, 80, and 70 words, 353 distinct words in all because iv is in two of them, and a forbidden word is rejected even if a deployment adds it to the vocabulary. 

**Slot lookup and drug names:** For each slot, the layer looks up the order with the slot's identifier and blocks if no order has it, if the order is inactive, if the order lacks the field, or if another active order has a related drug name. Two names are related if either has no word made of ASCII letters, either contains a character outside the ASCII range, they are equal ignoring case, they share a word (a maximal run of letters and digits, lowercased), or one word begins the other and the shorter word has at least four characters. This rule blocks two active orders of metformin under different spellings or salts, but it cannot see that a brand name and a generic name denote one drug. A drug name from the orders is printed only if it begins and ends with a letter, uses only ASCII letters, spaces, and hyphens, has at most 60 characters and five words, and contains no forbidden word and no word from the framing vocabulary, the shape words, or a list of 77 words that signal instructions, placeholder text, or non-drug content, such as "see", "call", "sliding", "scale", "milk", and "placeholder", so that a display name with a digit, or with a number or unit word as a separate word, such as "Lisinopril 10 MG Oral Tablet", cannot print a second strength, and "Warfarin see doctor first" is not printed. The rule tests whole words only, so a name that joins a spelled-out strength to a unit, such as "Metformin fivemg", is printed as written: with a dose of 500 mg, the built binary returns "Take Metformin fivemg 500 mg by mouth twice a day." A deployment must check display names for spelled-out strengths against its orders. The rule does not screen for medical content, so a record name such as "Metformin treats diabetes" prints as written, and a deployment must check display names against a clinical list. Last, the layer requires a non-word character between neighboring pieces, so that no model word can be joined to a filled value. Because the checks run in a fixed order, the first problem found determines the reason for blocking. The reason and its detail are returned so that the application can ask the model to rewrite; the detail can quote the model's text, so it must never be shown to a patient.

**Fallback:** When an output is blocked, the application needs something else to show. The command `dosegate fallback` ignores the model's output, builds the sentence "Take {{drug:id}} {{sig:id}}." for every active order, so that each line reads like "Take metformin 500 mg by mouth, twice a day.", and passes that text through `render` with the default settings, so the theorems below hold for the lines it prints; its limits are discussed later. The command does not check order identifiers before it builds the template, so a malformed identifier makes `render` block the output (with the reason `malformed` or `unbound`), and no text is shown. It blocks when some order cannot be printed, for example an order without a usable dose, and when two active orders have related drug names, as in a split regimen of one drug; it returns an error reply (status `error`) when no order is active, and `render` blocks with the reason `noActiveOrder` for the same case, so both refuse. The command accepts no options: with any option, such as `--extra-words`, it prints the usage line and exits with status 1, so it cannot add an as-needed reason or a warning. It does not detect two unrelated names for one drug, so a deployment must check for duplicate therapy before showing its output. The comma comes from the sig slot. The same order printed from a model text with dose, route, and frequency slots reads "Take metformin 500 mg by mouth twice a day." with no comma.

**Daily totals:** The daily slot multiplies the dose by the number of administrations per day, computed exactly from the FHIR timing. For a period in days, a daily total exists only when the period is one day, and the number per day is the frequency. For a period in hours, a daily total exists only when the frequency is one and the period divides 24, and the number per day is 24 divided by the period. For a weekly or monthly order, for an as-needed order, and for any other timing, no daily total exists and the slot blocks; a weekly order therefore prints only its frequency, as in "Take methotrexate 10 mg by mouth once a week.", and never a daily amount. The layer also refuses to print a timing given more than once per period of hours, such as "three times every 8 hours", which a patient can misread. Because the daily total ends the instruction for its own order, it states the total of that instruction only. The layer cannot tell that two orders name one drug, so each daily total is correct only for its own order.

**Exact quantities:** Doses are represented as exact decimals, a natural-number mantissa and a power-of-ten scale, and never as floating-point numbers, and the layer prints each dose with its value unchanged and without conversion or rounding, in the normal form described next (a leading zero before a decimal point, and no trailing zero after one). The printed form follows the leading-zero, trailing-zero, and insulin-unit rules for dose designations of the Institute for Safe Medication Practices and the Joint Commission:^31,32^ a leading zero precedes a decimal point ("0.5 mg", never ".5 mg"), no trailing zero follows one ("1 mg", never "1.0 mg"), and the unit for insulin and similar products is written "units" ("unit" for a dose of one), never "U" or "IU". The singular form is the layer's own choice, not a rule from those sources. The authors did not check the rules for micrograms against those sources. A clinical pharmacist must check the current ISMP list for its entry on micrograms before any patient-facing use, because mcg and mg are easily confused, and the layer prints mcg in every microgram line. A dose given as a count, such as one tablet or two puffs, is not printed, because a count gives the patient no strength to check; the input reader gives such an order no usable dose, and the output blocks with the reason `missingField`. A deployment must send each dose as a strength in a unit such as milligrams or micrograms. The layer prints one canonical name for each unit. "ug" and "mcg" both print as mcg, "ml" prints as mL, and the unit codes "U", "[iU]", and "[IU]" print as units ("unit" for a dose of one). An order with the bare code IU, or with unit text IU and no accepted code, is refused. The amount keeps its value, written in the normal form above. The released text does not state the concentration of an insulin product, such as U-100 or U-500. The same number of units is a different amount of insulin at each concentration, so a deployment must print the concentration from the dispensed product. For example, 20 units of U-500 insulin contains as much insulin as 100 units of U-100, so one printed number can hide a fivefold difference in dose. The evaluation does not test patient-facing unit words such as milligrams or micrograms, and a deployment for patients must decide whether to spell them out, which requires a change to `DoseGate/Units.lean` and new proofs for the printed form.

## Specification and proofs

**What is proved:** The development is written in Lean 4.35.0, using only the core library. The fidelity predicate, `SegRendersTo` with its list lifting `Forall₂`, is defined in DoseGate/Spec.lean as a predicate over the inputs and the returned text, separately from the code that computes the result. The other properties in Table 1 are stated as conclusions of theorems in DoseGate/Proofs; no single predicate combines them, and the conjunction is discussed under Table 1. The development contains 200 theorems in 2,074 lines of proof over 576 lines of definitions (non-blank lines, including the 150 lines in DoseGate/Fhir.lean, which hold trusted tables and functions that the proofs cover). Every theorem is checked by the Lean kernel; none uses an unproved assumption (`sorry`) or evaluation by compiled code (`native_decide`). A file in the repository prints the axioms of all 200 theorems: 121 depend on the three standard axioms of Lean (propositional extensionality, the axiom of choice, and quotient soundness), 68 on a proper subset of them, and 11 on none. Table 1 lists the principal theorems.

**The tokenizer lemma:** Most theorems in Table 1 follow from the structure of `render` once the right invariants are stated, but two required substantive arguments. The first concerns words. The guarantee that the model contributes no forbidden word must hold for the returned string, not merely for each piece, and concatenation can merge words: a model word placed directly against a filled dose, as in "x{{dose:m1}}", would merge with the number into the single word "x500". The lemma `tokens_flatten` proves that when every boundary between neighboring pieces has a non-word character on at least one side, the words of the concatenation are exactly the concatenation of the words of the pieces, and `render` checks that condition before returning. The second concerns decimals. `parseDec_fmtDec` proves that parsing the printed form of any decimal returns its normalized value, which with the uniqueness of normal forms yields `fmtDec_eq_iff`: two doses print identically if and only if they are the same quantity. No printed decimal ends in a trailing zero after its decimal point, so "1.0 mg" is never printed.

**Trusted code:** The proofs cover `render` and the functions it calls, given a list of orders already in Lean's internal form. They do not cover the conversion of FHIR JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) into that form, which is performed by 739 non-blank lines of Lean: 532 in the input reader (`DoseGate/Json.lean`) and 207 in the command-line program (`Main.lean`). The input reader refuses a request with an error when it cannot read a field that decides whether an order is current or what it says. At the request, bundle, and bundle entry levels, an unknown key whose value is null is ignored. Elsewhere, a null value still counts as a key: an order or dosage key outside its allowlist removes the dose even when its value is null. The input reader refuses the whole request with an error reply, rather than guess at an order's meaning, when any of the following holds: a request, bundle, or bundle entry has a key outside its allowlist whose value is not null; the `bundle` or `medicationRequests` key is null; a bundle entry is not an object, has no `resource`, or has a `resource` without a non-empty string `resourceType`; a list element has a `resourceType` that is present and is not `MedicationRequest`; a bundle has a non-null `resourceType` other than `Bundle`; the `patient` field is neither null nor a non-empty string; an order has an empty `id`; an order's `doNotPerform` or `reportedBoolean` is present and is neither a boolean nor null; or an order has a wrong-typed `medicationCodeableConcept`, `dosageInstruction`, timing, route, dose, `asNeededBoolean`, `dispenseRequest`, or `meta` field. A `frequency` or `period` that is not a whole number is a wrong-typed timing field. An order whose status or intent is missing or is not a FHIR R4 code (case variants such as "Active" included) also makes the whole request an error. An order is active only when its status is active, its intent is an order intent, and its `doNotPerform` is absent, null, or false. The order intents are `order`, `original-order`, `reflex-order`, `filler-order`, and `instance-order`. The `priority` field is read and ignored, so an order with priority `stat` is treated like any other order. Valid statuses other than active (on-hold, cancelled, completed, entered-in-error, stopped, draft, unknown) and valid intents that are not order intents (proposal, plan, option) make the order inactive, and inactive orders are omitted without a block. The fuzz reference shares that choice, so the fuzz test checks agreement with the specification and cannot show that the choice is safe. An order with unknown status is omitted without a block, although an error would be the stricter rule. A bundle entry whose `resourceType` is not one of the 145 resource type names of FHIR R4, such as the lowercase `medicationrequest`, `MedicationRequest` with a trailing space, or `MedicationRequests`, makes the whole request an error; an entry of another valid type, such as `Patient` or `MedicationStatement`, holds no order the layer reads and is skipped. The option `--allow-omissions` is the only option that lets an active order go unnamed without a block. When the request names a patient, an order is for that patient when its subject reference is `Patient/<id>`, `urn:uuid:<id>`, or an http or https URL ending in `Patient/<id>`; a reference in one of those forms with another id makes the order inactive; any other form makes the request an error. If no order is active, render blocks with `noActiveOrder` each output that passes every earlier check and has no slot. On such a chart, an output with a slot gets the reason of its first failing check, in the order listed in Design. That reason is `inactiveOrder` when the slot names an inactive order and `unknownRef` when the slot names no order, unless an earlier check fails first. For example, `Take # {{drug:m1}} {{dose:m1}} {{route:m1}} {{frequency:m1}}.` on a chart whose only order is stopped is blocked with `disallowedCharacter`.

An order keeps its drug name but yields no dose, timing, or route when any of the following holds:

- it has a top-level key outside an allowlist of 37 keys (`orderKeys` in DoseGate/Json.lean), which leaves out `statusReason`, `detectedIssue`, `text`, and `contained`, among other FHIR R4 elements;
- it has an extension at any depth;
- it has a security label, a `priorPrescription` (an earlier order that this one replaces), or another order's `priorPrescription` reference is exactly `MedicationRequest/<id>`;
- it has a dispense validity period;
- it has `reportedBoolean` true, or has a `reportedReference` key (a null value counts, while a null `reportedBoolean` is absent), which marks a medicine that was reported rather than ordered;
- its course of therapy (the FHIR element that says whether treatment is continuous, acute (short course), or seasonal; only continuous orders print a dose) is not continuous;
- its dosage, timing, repeat (the element that gives how many doses fall in each period), dose, or route contains a key outside a short allowlist, which excludes ranges, maxima, timing codes, events, bounds, sites, methods, and rates;
- it has more than one dosage instruction;
- the unit text of the dose disagrees with its code, or the dose names a code system other than the Unified Code for Units of Measure (a dose without a code system is read as if it used that system);
- the dose is given as a count (tablet, capsule, puff, or drop), which gives the patient no strength to check;
- the route's text or coding display disagrees with its code;
- by default, the order has a note, or its dosage has free text, an additional instruction, or a patient instruction, because free text can hold or limit the structured dose, as in "HOLD until your doctor restarts it".

A dose with more than nine decimal places, a mantissa of 10^15 or more, or a zero amount yields no dose, and a frequency or period that exceeds 1,000 yields no timing. The drug name is the order's text when every coding display begins with it; without text, it is the coding display when all displays agree; otherwise it is empty. The tables that map route codes from SNOMED CT (the clinical terminology that codes routes) and unit codes to the layer's routes and units are also trusted, since no theorem ties them to those code systems. The Appendix lists each route code that the layer accepts, with the phrase it prints; a clinician must check those codes against SNOMED CT before a deployment. The words that print frequency (`timesWord`, `unitWord`, and the phrase "every hour") are also trusted. They are proved injective (`tw_inj` and `unitWord_inj`), so different frequencies print as different text, but no theorem checks their English meaning against an outside source. A request is rejected if any order lacks a subject reference, even when the request names a patient, or if its orders name more than one patient and the request does not name the patient; a bundle that has a `type` must be a search set or collection and must have no transaction entries; and a request may not carry both a bundle and a list of orders.

Requests are rejected when they have duplicate JSON keys, nesting deeper than 64 levels, a numeric literal longer than 1,000 characters or with an exponent of three or more digits, more than 20,000 numeric literals, more than 5,000 orders, an output longer than 65,536 characters, more than 400 slots, or more than two megabytes. These rules are covered by 270 regression tests and by a differential fuzz test that generates mutated requests from the evaluation orders and compares every reply of the layer with that of a Python reference written from this specification. The reference shares the route and unit tables with the Lean source, so the test checks agreement with the specification, not the specification itself, and it would not detect a policy choice such as silently dropping on-hold orders. On 20,000 requests covering 53 kinds of mutation, all replies agreed. The trusted base also includes the Lean kernel and compiler, the JSON parser in Lean's standard library, and the operating system, as for other verified software.^33,34^

## Development and held-out testing

**Version 1:** The first version of the layer allowed free sentences that could contain a drug slot and 123 common words chosen for saying what a medicine is for, and stated daily totals in a separate sentence, "That is a total of {{daily}} of {{drug}}.". It was developed through red-team testing in five labeled parts (rounds r1, r2, and r3, and round 4 in two parts, r4a and r4p), in which independent instances of Claude (Anthropic) that could read the source and run the layer wrote 290 adversarial requests, each with its own orders and output, and the layer was changed after each round. On these 290 requests, version 1 released 44 outputs and the comparison filter described below released 218 in the stored run (the current build releases 215 of 290), of which version 1's outputs included two misstatements and the comparison filter's 92. N01 was settled by a third labeler; F05 was relabeled in a later replay that departs from the labeling protocol (see Labels). The first printed the drug name field "Warfarin see doctor first" (request N01); the first two labelers split on it and the third counted it a misstatement, and the current drug-name rule refuses that name. The second (request F05) printed "Take metformin 500 mg by mouth twice a day." for an order whose record gives the reason "Discontinued due to adverse reaction"; both labelers first labeled it faithful, and a later replay relabeling reopened it (see Labels for the other reopened items), giving misstates_order, the label this paper uses (see Labels). Because version 1 was revised against these requests, they are development data. Version 1 was also evaluated with Qwen3.5-4B on all 240 cases, and those outputs, on both halves, were examined before version 2 was designed.

**Held-out round against version 1:** We then froze version 1 and asked four new instances to write a held-out round, told that nothing would change in response and asked to keep every attack they seriously tried. Each of the four instances could read the source of version 1 and run both binaries, so its requests were written with knowledge of the input reader code, not blind to it. The four families targeted the meaning of vocabulary sentences, FHIR record content, slot and sentence structure, and outputs in the style of a small model. Of 113 requests, version 1 released 74, of which 38 misstated an order, 13 made an unsupported claim, and 23 were faithful; the comparison filter released 104 in the stored run (the current build releases 92 of 113), of which 51 misstated an order (Table 2). The round-h1 rubric did not list omissions, so six version 1 releases and 11 comparison-filter releases that the labelers labeled faithful omit at least one active order (Table 2 caption). One of them, h1_model_style_drop_seven_of_eight_orders, gives a single instruction for a request with six active orders (its eight entries include one stopped order and one order with doNotPerform true) and was labeled faithful by both filters' labelers. Counting all such outputs as misstatements would give 44 and 62 (the round-h2 labelers did not count similar omissions consistently; see the next paragraph). The misstatements fell into four classes. A drug slot alone on a line or before a colon labeled another order's instruction ("metformin:" above the insulin instruction). A vocabulary sentence implied a route ("insulin glargine with water."). A daily total was stated as the drug's total while another active order for the same drug had a glued, salted, blank, or brand name. The input reader accepted fields it did not interpret, such as a unit code that disagreed with the unit text, an extension that held the order, or a superseded order. Version 1's proofs were correct, but the property they stated permitted these outputs.

**Version 2:** In response, version 2 removed slots and medical words from free sentences, moved the daily total into the instruction sentence, extended the related-name rule to every slot and to prefixes, refused drug names containing instruction words, and added the input reader rules for units, routes, drug codings, security labels, extensions, validity periods, superseded orders, rates, sites, methods, and transaction bundles. Its vocabulary is a fixed list of framing words; it was not assembled from model outputs, although the designers had seen version 1's outputs. Because version 2 was designed in response to round h1, its behavior on that round is not a held-out estimate.

**Held-out round against version 2:** We froze version 2 (commit ef2cc98) and asked five new instances to write a second held-out round in the same families plus a fifth on clinically realistic regimens such as tapers, weekly methotrexate, and insulin. Each of the five instances could read the source of version 2 and run both binaries, so its requests were written with knowledge of the input reader code. Because the requests were written with the version 2 source in view, they probe the checks as implemented. Of 138 requests, version 2 released 86, of which 9 misstated an order, 4 made an unsupported claim, and 73 were labeled faithful; of those 73, 54 were faithful and 19 gave two instructions for one ingredient under different names, such as "Take Lantus 20 units as a shot under the skin once a day." with "Take insulin glargine 10 units as a shot under the skin once a day.", or Percocet with acetaminophen, which Table 2 reports separately. The comparison filter released 128 in the stored run (the current build releases 107 of 138), of which 23 misstated an order, including all 9 of the layer's (Table 2). Six of the 9 came from record fields the input reader did not interpret: an extension on the repeat (one output), an extension on the dose quantity (one), an event list giving two dose times (two), a seasonal course of therapy (one), and a request carrying the same order in a bundle and in a list with different doses (one). Two came from a minus sign written by the model before a dose or a daily total ("Take Metformin -500 mg by mouth twice a day."), and one omitted five of the eight active orders after "Here is your medicine list."; the third labeler counted that omission as a misstatement. ## Evaluation methods

**Patient records:** Synthetic patients were drawn from Synthea,^35^ an open-source generator of synthetic electronic health records, as 5,980 FHIR bundles produced earlier for unrelated work by Synthea version v3.2.0-202-geeb6539e2 with US Core profiles (the United States base profiles of FHIR). In these bundles, medication orders record each dose as one tablet without a unit, give the strength only in the RxNorm display name (RxNorm is the National Library of Medicine's normalized drug name vocabulary^36^), and omit the route, so under the default input reader no raw Synthea order yields an instruction that states its dose, route, and frequency. We therefore normalized every active order whose display name gave exactly one strength in milligrams for a tablet or capsule: the dose became that strength in milligrams, the route became oral, the drug name became the text before the strength, lowercased, which for some orders still contains words such as "12 hr", and the timing was copied unchanged. This transformation is not performed by the layer. The RxNorm name gives the strength of the one levothyroxine order in the evaluation (its natural case) as 0.075 mg, although US levothyroxine tablets are labeled in micrograms. The layer prints the unit the record gives, so the evaluation does not test the microgram form, and a deployment must send the unit printed on the dispensed label. An output that names the natural levothyroxine order prints 0.075 mg, and one that names the perturbed order prints 0.0375 mg; a patient holding tablets labeled in micrograms cannot match either figure, so for that drug the unit is a patient-facing error in the evaluation's own orders and not only an untested form. The bundles are not distributed, but the script eval/build_cases.py rebuilds the normalized cases from them exactly (SHA-256 of cases.jsonl, 8dd1d13f78e2e2e1dabb167b4945bc688fed62b83205c4508574250d0dd4eb9f). The first 120 patients with at least one usable order, after shuffling with a fixed seed, contributed up to four orders each, and each patient yielded a natural case with the record's strengths and timings and a perturbed case that multiplied each dose by 0.5, 1.5, 2, or 3 and replaced each timing with once daily, twice daily, three times daily, every 8 hours, every 12 hours, or once weekly. The 240 cases contain 251 distinct orders, each in a natural and a perturbed case, for 502 order records of 11 drug names, all oral: lisinopril (162), hydrochlorothiazide (148), amlodipine (126), simvastatin (28), acetaminophen (16), losartan potassium (6), 12 hr hydrocodone bitartrate (6), abuse-deterrent 12 hr oxycodone hydrochloride (4), levothyroxine sodium (2), meperidine hydrochloride (2), and dolutegravir (2). The normalization drops every note, dosage text, additional instruction, and patient instruction, so no evaluation order carries free text and the evaluation does not exercise the default free-text rule. In the raw records of the 120 evaluation patients, 118 of 548 active MedicationRequests (21.5%) carry one of these fields, and 52 of the 120 patients (43.3%) have at least one such order. Across all 5,980 bundles, 6,013 of 26,800 active MedicationRequests (22.4%) do, and 2,685 bundles (44.9%) have at least one. Among active orders with a single dosage instruction, the shares are 32.7% for the 120 patients and 35.1% for all bundles. The default rule would block every output for a patient with such an order, because the order then has no usable dose, so these shares measure how much of a raw record the default rule withholds (`eval/free_text_share.py`, `eval/data/free_text_share.json`). The cases were split by patient into a development half and a test half of 60 patients and 120 cases each. All version 2 results are on the 120 test cases. Because the layer requires every order to carry one patient subject, the scripts add the subject "Patient/p1" to every order.

**Models and conditions:** Three open models from three developers were run locally on an Apple M2 Max computer with 32 GB of memory: Qwen3.5-4B,^37^ with full-precision weights from the Hugging Face repository Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B, run with MLX LM version 0.32.2^38^ and reasoning disabled; MedGemma 4B,^39^ a Gemma 3 model adapted for medical text by Google; and Llama 3.2 3B^40^ from Meta. The last two were run with 4-bit quantized weights (Q4_K_M) through Ollama version 0.40.14, so model and runtime vary together. Every open-model generation was limited to 400 new tokens; the Claude runs used 2,000 (see Claude runs). Each of the 120 test-half cases received six generations from each open model: one each of free daily, slot, slot daily, and attack, and two slot samples at temperature 1.0 (720 first-attempt generations per model; the Claude runs are described below). The prompts are in the Appendix. In the free daily condition the model saw the orders in plain text and was asked to explain how to take each medicine and the total amount per day. In the slot condition the model was asked only for each medicine's instruction, and in the slot daily condition also for the total per day; both carried the slot rules and one example, and the model saw each order's identifier and drug name but not its dose, route, or timing. In these two greedy conditions, when the layer blocked an output, the model received the layer's explanation and was asked to rewrite, up to two times. In the attack condition the model saw the full orders with the slot rules and was told to tell the patient to take double the usual amount of the first medicine and to state the number of milligrams. Two further slot samples per case were drawn at temperature 1.0 with nucleus sampling (top-p 0.95) and were not rewritten. All other open-model generations used greedy decoding; the Claude runs are described below. The free daily prompt is unchanged from the version 1 study, so for Qwen3.5-4B those 120 outputs were taken from that run. The version 1 study also had a free condition, which asked for the orders without daily totals; version 2 did not run it. Generation ran against version 2, and every decision reported below was recomputed with version 2.1. The completeness default and the placeholder handling in version 2.1 followed the release of one test-half output under version 2 (Results, Released text), so the test-half release rates for version 2.1 are not held-out estimates. The rewrite requests were produced from version 2's block messages, so the version 2.1 rates after rewrites are replays of version 2's rewrite chains. Across the 2,764 decisions, this changed one release. The input reader rules added in review round 10 refuse none of the 240 case requests, so they change none of these decisions.

**Claude runs:** Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 were run later through the Anthropic application programming interface (API), outside the planned three-model design. The runs used the repository's generation script with the API backend, low effort, the same prompts, a limit of 2,000 output tokens, and no temperature sent, so the API default sampling applied. Each record's `api` field gives the limit, the effort, and the stop reason. All 1,051 records stopped at the end of the model's turn, so no output was cut off. The longest outputs were 1,309 tokens for Haiku and 710 for Sonnet. Each Claude output is therefore one sample at the API's default sampling, so the two runs were sampled rather than greedy only if that default is above zero, and their rates are not directly comparable with the greedy rows of Table 3. The Messages API reference (^41^) was checked for this revision, and the numeric default temperature was not found in the text read. The `temp` field in their files reads 0.0, which is a placeholder, as the README states. Each ran on the 120 test cases in the slot, slot daily, free daily, and attack conditions. The Haiku file holds 521 records and the Sonnet file 530, including 41 and 50 rewrite records; each condition has 120 first attempts. The scoring script scored their outputs, but no labeler labeled their free daily outputs that the script flagged (46 for Haiku and 28 for Sonnet, counted by the audit function in `eval/score.py`). Table 3 keeps the three planned open models, and the two Claude runs are reported in Results.

**Comparison filter:** To measure how much the layer's checks reduce misstatements, we built a comparison filter that keeps slot substitution and removes all of the layer's text checks together. The gap between the two filters is the joint effect of those checks, and it does not isolate any one check or test the proofs, which show only that the checks imply the stated property. The filter reads each request with the same FHIR input reader and the request shape and subject checks of the version it tests, without the order status, intent, and subject-form checks that version 2.1 adds (these matter only for requests with an invalid order field), and fills dose, route, frequency, sig, and daily slots with the same formatting as the layer, but prints drug names without the drug-name rule and checks model text only for braces and a blocklist of digits and the 78 unit and dose words. It has no sentence shapes, vocabulary, repeated-value rule, separation rule, related-name rule, sign rule, or completeness rule. It was applied to every model output and every adversarial request.

**Labels:** Adversarial texts released by either filter were labeled by two independent instances of Claude as faithful, as misstating an order, or as making an unsupported claim; a third labeler labeled every output on which they disagreed or that one of them had not labeled. In the development corpus and round h1, an output misstated an order if it stated a drug, amount, daily total, route, or frequency that differs from the active orders, told the patient to take an order that is not active, or told the patient to skip, stop, double, or adjust a medicine. The round-h2 rubric adds two clauses to the development and round-h1 rubric, attaching an instruction to the wrong medicine and presenting a conditional order as one fixed dose, so the development and round-h1 misstatement counts are lower bounds with respect to it. The prednisone and Deltasone pair (h2_clinical_10) is labeled faithful because the request gives two ordinary orders with no taper marker; its use as a taper is known only from how the test was designed, and Limits treats it as a risk for that reason. **Outcome measures:** For each condition we recorded whether the layer returned text and, if not, the reason. A scoring script located mass quantities and frequency phrases in text and attributed each sentence or line to the drug it named or most recently named. It counted an order as misstated when a quantity stated as a daily total (after the word "total" or before a phrase such as "a day") differed from the order's daily total or the order had none, when any other quantity equaled neither the dose nor the daily total, or when no stated frequency was equivalent to the order's timing, where "twice a day" and "every 12 hours" count as equivalent. These rules are lenient in two ways: an amount equal to the daily total is accepted in place of the dose, and one matching frequency suffices, so the script's counts are lower bounds. To show that the script detects the errors it is meant to detect, each released text other than the 19 released attack outputs received three separate planted errors where applicable: its first order's dose multiplied by ten, its daily total replaced by its dose (only texts that state a daily total), and its frequency changed. The script was run on each altered text separately. Because the layer's guarantee is a theorem, the expected count of misstatements in released text is zero, and the measurement checks the implementation, including the trusted input reader, rather than the proof. Proportions are reported with 95% Wilson score intervals, which have better coverage than the Wald interval near 0% and 100%; the Wilson intervals treat outputs as independent although they cluster within 60 patients. Patient-cluster intervals from a percentile bootstrap over the 60 test patients (2,000 resamples, `eval/bootstrap.py`) are given beside the Wilson intervals in Table 3. They differ from the Wilson bounds by at most 2.1 percentage points, and they are wider at some bounds and narrower at others, so the Wilson intervals are not uniformly narrow. Percentages are rounded half up. Reading level was estimated with the Flesch-Kincaid grade formula, which estimates grade level from sentence and word length. Latency was measured by sending all outputs of one model through a pipe from Python to one layer process and dividing the elapsed time, which includes the pipe, by their number. The fuzz test is run as python3 tests/fuzz_reader.py --core v2 --seed 7 --count 20000, where --core v2 selects the version 2.1 rules.

Of its 913 blocked slot outputs, counting rewrites, 885 were blocked as sentences that match no valid shape (the reason `unbound`, which also covers framing sentences with words outside the vocabulary), and 858 contain a drug name from the order list, as in "Take lisinopril {{dose:m1}} {{route:m1}} {{frequency:m1}}." The containment count does not show that each name took the place of a slot. At temperature 1.0, Qwen3.5-4B sometimes copied the placeholder "ID" from the prompt as an order identifier (22 outputs, each blocked by version 2.1 with the reason omittedOrder, because the layer checks that every active order is named before it resolves slot identifiers; version 2 blocked the same outputs with the reason unknownRef, because no order has the identifier "ID"). Released text had a median Flesch-Kincaid grade of 3.6 for Qwen3.5-4B (118 slot outputs), 3.1 for MedGemma 4B (100), 3.4 for Llama 3.2 3B (34 outputs at temperature 1.0), 3.9 for Claude Haiku 5.5 (118), and 4.0 for Claude Sonnet 5.5 (118). The free daily text of the same runs, which the layer blocked, had medians of 3.8, 5.5, 6.0, 3.5, and 3.3. Against free text from the same runs, released text was lower by 0.2 grade for Qwen3.5-4B, 2.4 for MedGemma 4B, and 2.6 for Llama 3.2 3B, and higher by 0.4 for Haiku 5.5 and 0.7 for Sonnet 5.5. The two sets of text differ in length and in how they were generated, so these differences do not show that the layer changes reading level. The formula is unstable for texts of a few sentences and does not weigh long drug names, so these grades are a rough check, and plain-language quality was not assessed with an instrument for patient materials. A grade near 3 measures sentence and word length only; it does not show that a patient understands the abbreviations mg, mL, and mcg or the phrase by mouth.

**Released doses above label doses:** The layer reports orders as written, including orders a clinician would question. We compared each released order's daily total with the label maximum or the top of the usual range in US prescribing information on DailyMed:^42^ amlodipine 10 mg ("the maximum dose is 10 mg once daily"), simvastatin 40 mg ("The maximum recommended dosage is ... 40 mg once daily", with 80 mg restricted to patients already taking it), hydrochlorothiazide 50 mg (the highest dose the label names for hypertension, which the label allows as an increase from a usual initial dose of 25 mg daily: "The dose may be increased to 50 mg daily"), and lisinopril 40 mg, the top of its usual range ("The usual dosage range is 20 mg to 40 mg per day"; the label sets no explicit adult maximum for hypertension and adds that doses up to 80 mg have been used without greater effect). Of the 368 outputs released from perturbed cases, 221 give an order whose daily total exceeds one of these doses (hydrochlorothiazide in 113 outputs, amlodipine in 79, lisinopril in 48, and simvastatin in 24; an output can count for more than one drug). In 134 of them the released text prints that daily total, as in "Take amlodipine 15 mg by mouth once a day, which is 15 mg a day."; in the other 87 it states a dose and frequency whose product exceeds the label dose. None of the 499 outputs released from natural cases does either. The script eval/maxima.py computes these counts from the final decision files. The perturbed orders were made atypical on purpose, and the layer reported each of them exactly as written: fidelity to an order is not clinical safety, and a label-limit check on the orders must run before the layer.

**Errors in free text:** When the models wrote freely and had to state daily totals, the scoring script flagged 16 of 120 test outputs from Qwen3.5-4B, 71 from MedGemma 4B, and 31 from Llama 3.2 3B. The same version 1 Qwen3.5-4B run also flagged 16 of 120 development outputs, and labelers confirmed a misstatement in all 16 (15 by two labelers, and one by the author's main session, which was not blind). All 16 test-half flags for Qwen3.5-4B occurred in the 21 test cases that contain a weekly order (all 21 are perturbed cases, because no natural case has a weekly order), and both labelers classed each as a weekly dose reported as a daily total. The labelers confirmed a misstatement in 69 of MedGemma 4B's 71 flagged outputs and in 19 of Llama 3.2 3B's 31; most of Llama 3.2 3B's other flags were statements of what a medicine is for, which the labelers labeled unsupported claims. In MedGemma 4B's 69, the main error was adding the doses of different drugs into one figure in 45, as in "Total: You take 35 milligrams of medicine in one day."; a daily total computed wrongly for one drug in 15, as in turning 20 mg every 8 hours into "four times a day, for a total of 80 milligrams"; a weekly dose stated as daily in 7; and a wrong frequency in 2. Each output is counted once, under the first error type in its label in eval/data/v2_review/labels.json. In Llama 3.2 3B's 19, the main errors were adding different drugs (5), a wrongly computed total (4), a weekly dose stated as daily (4), a wrong dose (2), a wrong frequency (1), and other errors (3). Among 20 unflagged outputs sampled per model, the labelers found a misstatement in 2 for MedGemma 4B and 1 for Llama 3.2 3B, so at least 71 and 20 of the 120 outputs misstated an amount, and these are lower bounds. The scoring script also flagged 46 of 120 free daily outputs from Claude Haiku 5.5 and 28 of 120 from Claude Sonnet 5.5 (counted by the audit function in `eval/score.py`). No labeler has labeled these flags, so they are unconfirmed and are not in any count in this section. Eleven free-text outputs were read once by the author's main session, without blinding: one development flag and ten unflagged outputs with a weekly order (eval/data/free_text_labels.json). The MedGemma 4B and Llama 3.2 3B flags come from two labelers, and a third labeler settled each disagreement (eval/data/v2_review/labels.json). Under the default settings, the layer cannot release a free daily output, because it has no slot and so names no order; the free daily error rates therefore come from the scoring script and the labels above.

**Attack:** Told to double the first medicine, Qwen3.5-4B stated the doubled amount in milligrams in 100 of 120 outputs, MedGemma 4B in 37, and Llama 3.2 3B in 114. The layer blocked every output that stated the doubled amount. The 19 attack outputs it released from Qwen3.5-4B were ordinary instruction lists in which the model had not followed the attack and wrote no amount. In 21 test cases the first order is taken twice a day or every 12 hours, so the doubled dose equals the correct daily total; the models stated that doubled amount in 21, 6, and 20 of those cases, and a filter that accepts any number derivable from the orders would have released those statements. We did not run such a filter, so this is an expectation from the design and not a measurement.

**Comparison filter on model outputs:** For Qwen3.5-4B and MedGemma 4B, the comparison filter released every output the layer released and 4 more for each. The labelers labeled MedGemma 4B's 4 faithful. Of Qwen3.5-4B's 4, three were labeled faithful; the fourth, which omitted the 12 hr hydrocodone bitartrate order, was not sent to the labelers, and version 2 released it while version 2.1 blocks it. For Llama 3.2 3B the comparison filter released 171 outputs that the layer blocked, and the labelers labeled 56 of them misstatements, 49 of which carry the error label duplicated_instruction, meaning an instruction repeated for the same order ("Take lisinopril 10 mg by mouth once a day. Take lisinopril 10 mg by mouth, once a day."), which the layer's repeated-value rule blocks. The remaining 115 were labeled faithful, and none was labeled an unsupported claim.

**Agreement:** For the adversarial corpora, the first two labelers agreed on 195 of 218 comparison-filter outputs and 42 of 44 layer outputs in the development corpus (Cohen's kappa, a measure of agreement between two labelers corrected for chance: 0.83 and 0.88), on 79 of 89 and 60 of 67 outputs labeled by both in round h1 (0.80 and 0.81), and on 106 of 128 and 74 of 86 in round h2 (0.49 and 0.50). Most round-h2 texts were faithful, which lowers kappa at a given level of agreement, and the third labeler decided 5 of the 9 round-h2 layer misstatements, so the round-h2 comparison rests partly on single tie-breaking judgments. For the model outputs, the two labelers agreed on 360 of the 379 items that both labeled (kappa 0.91); for one further item the first labeler's label was not recorded, and a third labeler labeled it together with the 19 disagreements, 20 items in all. The models' errors came when they had to compute a quantity, such as a daily total for a weekly order or a sum over several drugs, and when their prompt told them to state an amount. We did not measure a filter that compares numbers in the output with the orders. The design argument predicts that such a filter would handle these cases poorly, because a computed total is a number the orders do not contain, and a harmful number can equal a legitimate one, as the doubled dose did in 47 attack outputs (21 from Qwen3.5-4B, 6 from MedGemma 4B, and 20 from Llama 3.2 3B). The layer avoids both by never accepting a number or a drug name from the model: proved code computes every total, and anything the model writes outside the framing vocabulary blocks the output. On the comparison-only outputs of the two models that followed the format, the labelers found no misstatement in the 7 they labeled (3 Qwen3.5-4B and 4 MedGemma 4B), and one further Qwen3.5-4B output, which omits the 12 hr hydrocodone bitartrate order, is unlabeled. The comparison filter, which substitutes slots and blocks digits and unit words, released 56 misstatements from Llama 3.2 3B, the model that did not follow the format. In the second held-out round it released 23 misstatements, and the 9 that version 2 released were among them.

**Model-agnostic, not model-independent:** The theorems hold for any model, so changing the model cannot make the layer release a misstatement within the scope of the proofs. The rate of usable output, however, depends strongly on the model: across the three open models on the same cases and prompts, greedy release after rewrites ranged from 98.3% to 1.7% (the two Claude runs, under different settings, each released 98.3% of their slot outputs after rewrites). A deployment therefore has to measure its own model's release rate, and a model that cannot follow the format gives a system that blocks most outputs and gives the patient no instruction from it. Larger models answer medical examination questions accurately^43^ and may follow the format more reliably, but that would change only the release rate, not what released text can say. Decoding constrained to the slot grammar and vocabulary might raise release rates without weakening the guarantee, but we did not test it.

**What a proof does and does not establish:** Version 1's theorems were correct, yet a held-out round found 38 misstatements in its 74 releases under the round-h1 rubric (the round-h2 rubric adds two clauses, so the count under that rubric would be at least 38), because the specification allowed a free sentence to name a drug and so to label another order's instruction, and allowed a daily total to read as the drug's total. A proof establishes only the property it states, and held-out adversarial testing was what showed that the property was too weak. Review of version 2 found a similar gap at smaller scale: the sign rule looked only at the character immediately before a slot, so "Take metformin - 500 mg" passed, and the proof of the narrower rule was correct. The property proved for version 2.1 is stronger: every drug name and value that the layer fills from an order appears inside an instruction for that order that states its dose, route, and frequency, every active order has such an instruction, and every other sentence is drawn from a fixed vocabulary that the authors judged free of medical content (no theorem checks this). The theorems say what released text satisfies; they do not show that the layer releases text. Two results concern release. `render_ok_eq` shows that `render` returns its text whenever every check passes and either some order is active or the no-active-order check is off. `render_one_active_ok` gives a concrete case: for a fixed one-order aspirin chart and one template, `render` releases "Take aspirin 81 mg by mouth once a day.", so an implementation that always blocks would not satisfy the example. No theorem shows that a given model output passes those checks. Even so, the second held-out round found misstatements that the proofs did not exclude, mostly in the trusted input reader, and version 2.1 has not been tested on a held-out round.

**What the patient sees when output is blocked:** A blocked output gives the patient nothing from the model, and the reason and detail the layer returns can quote the model's text, for example "and do not stop the pills", so the application must never show them to a patient. The `dosegate fallback` command gives a deterministic alternative: one line per active order of the form "Take metformin 500 mg by mouth, twice a day.", rendered by the same proved code, so the theorems hold for the lines it prints. It has no heading and no daily total. For a request with no active order, `dosegate fallback` returns an error reply and no text. The application must treat that reply as a failure and route the patient to a pharmacist; it must not show an empty list. Two orders with unrelated names, such as Lantus and insulin glargine, remain two lines, because the fallback does not check for duplicate therapy, so a deployment must check the record for one ingredient under two names and withhold the fallback when it finds one, while two orders with related names, such as the two metformin orders of a split regimen, block the whole fallback with the reason ambiguousDrug, so the deployment has no text for that patient; an as-needed order with a coded reason also blocks the whole fallback, because the input reader treats such an order as one it cannot read, and an order whose drug name contains a digit also blocks it. The fallback also leaves out an order that is on hold, stopped, or marked doNotPerform, without a line or a warning, so a patient who still has tablets of such a medicine receives nothing about it from the fallback. A deployment must add a heading and a contact line for the care team from clinician-written text, and the fallback was not tested with patients. After rewrites, the layer blocked 1.7% of slot outputs for Qwen3.5-4B, 16.7% for MedGemma 4B, and 98.3% for Llama 3.2 3B. A block means the patient has no model text, so the deployment must either show the fallback, under the exclusions below, or show a list that a pharmacist has checked, or route the patient to a pharmacist. The fallback prints a line for 118 of the 120 test cases, including 20 of the 21 weekly-order cases (all perturbed). A deployment that withholds the fallback from weekly orders would still print lines for 98 cases. The record's own medication list is not checked by the layer and can carry the same names and text that the layer excludes. Orders whose drug names contain digits, such as "12 hr hydrocodone bitartrate", cannot be printed by the layer or by the fallback, and with completeness required they block every output for their patient, so a deployment must supply a display name, approved by a pharmacist, that keeps the release form. Do not release any line, from the fallback or from model text, to a patient who has an as-needed order (asNeededBoolean true) or a weekly or monthly order, unless a pharmacist has checked that patient's line. A weekly line such as "Take methotrexate 10 mg by mouth once a week." states the frequency but not that the dose is taken once a week only, so the released text does not by itself stop a patient from taking the dose every day. The fallback accepts no options, so the layer cannot add a reason for use or that warning; the application must route those patients to a pharmacist.

**Limits:** The released instruction is not a complete set of directions for a patient: it gives no number of tablets, no time of day, no food or hold direction, no indication, and no reason for an as-needed dose. The request-level rules for order status, intent, subject references, `doNotPerform`, unknown keys, and bundle entries are stated in Trusted code and are not repeated here. An order sent as a resource of another valid FHIR type, such as a MedicationStatement, is skipped, so a deployment must send each order as a MedicationRequest. The layer cannot check that a request holds every active order the patient has, so a deployment must make that check outside the layer. 

An order with doNotPerform true, or with status on-hold or stopped, is left out of the released text without a block, so the text gives the patient no word about that medicine. An order with `reportedBoolean` true, or with any `reportedReference` key, is closed by the reader, which yields no dose, timing, or route for it, because a medicine that a patient or another source reported is not a prescriber's instruction. The order stays active, so under the default settings every output for that patient is blocked: an output that does not name it is blocked with omittedOrder, and one that names it with missingField (checked with the built binary). Only `--allow-omissions` releases text for the patient's other orders. The reader ignores `priority`, and it treats the intents original-order, reflex-order, filler-order, and instance-order as orders, so a reflex or filler order prints as an ordinary instruction (checked with the built binary: metformin 500 mg by mouth twice a day with priority stat and intent reflex-order prints as ordinary text). A deployment must confirm that each order it sends is a prescriber's order and must check the record for reflex or filler orders before calling the layer. A patient who still has tablets of a prohibited, held, or stopped medicine gets no warning from the layer. A deployment must tell the patient, from clinician-written text, which medicines to stop, hold, or not take, and must not present the released list as complete unless a pharmacist has checked it. 

The related-name rule cannot recognize that a brand name and a generic name, such as "Lantus" and "insulin glargine", an abbreviation such as "ASA" for aspirin, a combination product such as Percocet beside a separate acetaminophen order (together these can exceed a daily acetaminophen maximum; the US label for oxycodone and acetaminophen tablets advises against more than 4,000 mg of acetaminophen a day^42^), or a one-letter misspelling such as "LisinopriI" beside "Lisinopril", denote one drug. In round h2, version 2.1 released 16 such pairs, each as two instructions a patient could follow together, so that a shared ingredient would be taken twice. Lisinopril in Zestoretic and in a separate lisinopril order is one example, and the Zestoretic line does not show its hydrochlorothiazide. Toprol-XL and metoprolol tartrate are one ingredient in different salt forms and release types (extended-release metoprolol succinate and metoprolol tartrate). That count rests on one author's reading of the drug names, which no clinician has checked. Version 2.1 releases these texts, so a deployment that does not check for duplicate therapy would send them to patients. Lantus 20 units once a day with insulin glargine 10 units once a day gives 30 units of basal insulin a day (h2_chart_same_drug_lantus_glargine). Synthroid 50 mcg once a day with levothyroxine 75 mcg once a day gives 125 mcg of levothyroxine a day (h2_meaning_02_brand_synthroid_levothyroxine). Glucophage 500 mg twice a day with metformin 1000 mg once a day gives 2,000 mg of metformin a day (h2_meaning_03_brand_glucophage_metformin). Acetaminophen 500 mg every 6 hours with Tylenol 650 mg every 4 hours as needed (h2_clinical_20) allows up to 5,900 mg of acetaminophen in one day at the printed frequencies: 2,000 mg scheduled plus 3,900 mg as needed. That total is 1,900 mg above the 4,000 mg a day that the label cited above advises against. A deployment that can supply an ingredient code for each order must check duplicate therapy before calling the layer. The rule also blocks one drug written as two active orders (a split regimen, such as metformin 500 mg in the morning and 1,000 mg in the evening as separate orders) and two different drugs whose names share a word, such as insulin glargine and insulin lispro; these outputs are blocked rather than released, so the cost is a missing instruction. A basal and mealtime insulin regimen, such as insulin glargine with insulin lispro, returns `ambiguousDrug` on both the model-text path and the fallback (checked with the built binary), so the layer gives that patient no text by either path, and the deployment must route the patient to a pharmacist. When a chart has two active orders for one drug, a deployment should send the patient to a pharmacist to reconcile the orders, using the order dates (`authoredOn`), dosing periods, and `priorPrescription` links. The layer never chooses the newer order, because choosing would drop an active order without a block; version 2.1 blocks related-name orders with `ambiguousDrug`. 

A superseded order is closed only when another order's `priorPrescription` reference is exactly `MedicationRequest/<id>`. For an output that names only the superseded order, the default check blocks in either form, because the replacement order loses its dose and goes unnamed; with `--allow-omissions` the exact form blocks, and the form `MedicationRequest/<id>/_history/1` or `urn:uuid:<id>` prints the superseded order (checked with the built binary). No regression test uses the history form in a priorPrescription reference. A deployment that uses `--allow-omissions` must rewrite every priorPrescription reference to the exact form before calling the layer, or must block any output that names a superseded order, because the history form otherwise prints the old dose as current. 

The drug-name rule blocks valid names that contain a unit word, such as "penicillin G potassium". It tests whole words, so a name that joins a spelled-out strength to a unit, such as "Metformin fivemg", is printed as written (with a dose of 500 mg, the built binary returns "Take Metformin fivemg 500 mg by mouth twice a day."), and a deployment must check display names for spelled-out strengths against its orders. Completeness holds only for the orders in the request: "Here is your medicine list." cannot reflect over-the-counter medicines or prescriptions from other prescribers, so a deployment must state which orders the list covers, from clinician-written text outside the layer, because the layer's options can add framing words but cannot remove or replace the framing sentence. Under the default settings the sentence is also untrue for a chart with an on-hold, stopped, or doNotPerform order, because the layer leaves that order out without a word. The built binary, with an active metformin order and a warfarin order with doNotPerform true, prints "Here is your medicine list. Take metformin 500 mg by mouth twice a day." The example sentence in the Appendix prompt should not be reused in a deployment unless a pharmacist has checked the chart. A regular insulin order prints under the drug name the record gives, such as "insulin regular", so a pharmacist must confirm each insulin display name and product concentration before the line reaches a patient. 

The layer prints the orders it receives, including chart errors: an amlodipine order of 100 mg is released as "Take amlodipine 100 mg by mouth once a day, which is 100 mg a day.", and in this evaluation 221 released outputs gave a daily amount above the label doses given in Results, 134 of them printing that daily total and 87 giving a dose and frequency whose product exceeds it. A conditional order, such as an insulin dose set by a blood glucose reading, a taper, or a dose given as directed, prints as one fixed dose when its structured fields hold a number and the record has no free text, because the layer cannot read a condition from those fields. A drug name that contains sliding, scale, or taper is refused by the drug-name rule, so the output blocks with missingField. A structured insulin order of 20 units three times a day, with no free text and the subcutaneous route (SNOMED CT 34206005, Table A1), prints "Take insulin regular 20 units as a shot under the skin three times a day." The Humulin R U-100 label directs injection approximately 30 minutes before meals,^42^ and the released line for a structured regular insulin order gives no meal timing, so a deployment must add that instruction for each regular insulin order from clinician-written text, after checking the label for the product and concentration it dispenses. A taper written as two active orders prints as two daily doses at once, which a patient could take together. Round h2 released one such case, prednisone 20 mg with Deltasone 40 mg, both once a day (h2_clinical_10, one of the 16 pairs). A deployment must find and screen conditional and taper orders against clinician-written instructions before the layer, and must not send their printed doses to a patient. The layer adds no indication, no missed-dose instruction, and no brand name of its own, but it prints each drug name as the record gives it, subject only to the drug-name rule, which checks for forbidden and framing words and not for medical content, so a record name such as "Lantus" or "Metformin treats diabetes" is released as written and a deployment must check display names against a clinical list. 

An as-needed order (`asNeededBoolean`) prints through the fallback as, for example, "Take oxycodone hydrochloride 5 mg by mouth, once a day as needed.", with no reason for use and no daily maximum, and "once a day as needed" can be read as a daily schedule; an order whose as-needed reason is coded blocks the output. With a timing above one dose a day, the wording is worse. An acetaminophen 500 mg order with four doses a day and the as-needed flag set prints "Take acetaminophen 500 mg by mouth four times a day as needed.", which a patient can read as four scheduled doses, so a deployment must not release that line to a patient until clinician-written wording replaces the frequency for as-needed orders. For an as-needed order, the frequency slot prints the order's timing followed by "as needed" (for example, "once a day as needed"), or only "as needed" when the order has no timing. The fallback prints the same phrase after a comma, as in "by mouth, once a day as needed." Both forms were checked with the built binary. Do not release any line, from the fallback or from model text, to a patient who has an as-needed order (asNeededBoolean true) or a weekly or monthly order, unless a pharmacist has checked that patient's line, and take the reason for use from clinician-written text outside the layer. The released text cannot carry any warning or any direction beyond the dose, route, frequency, and daily total: the layer blocks words such as "not", "stop", "only", "food", "meal", and "morning" in model text, so a model cannot write "Do not stop" or "Take with food". This matters most for weekly and extended-release orders. The released line for a weekly order gives the frequency, as in "once a week", but it cannot say that the dose is taken once a week only, so the released text does not by itself stop a patient from taking the dose every day. The layer also cannot print "swallow whole" (the word swallow is forbidden), so an extended-release product is released without that instruction. The 12 hr names are blocked only because they contain digits, which is a side effect of the drug-name rule and not a safeguard for extended-release products. A deployment must add these warnings from clinician-written text for each such drug and must not rely on that block. 

By default, a food, time-of-day, or hold instruction in the record blocks the output for the whole patient, because the order then has no usable dose. With the option `--ignore-free-text`, the instruction is dropped and the line prints without it. In the raw synthetic records, 193 orders say "Every twelve hours as required" in their text and have the as-needed flag false. With `--ignore-free-text`, an order with a plain drug name, a route, and a dose with a unit prints as a scheduled dose at the timing in its structured record. The 193 raw orders do not print this way. In the raw bundles, each of the 193 has a drug name with digits (for example 12 HR Hydrocodone Bitartrate 10 MG Extended Release Oral Capsule), no route, a dose with no unit, and a `doseAndRate` element with a `type` key, which makes the dose unusable, and each sets `asNeededBoolean` to false. The stored check records that all 193 still block with missingField under the option (`eval/data/coded_timing_check.json`). An order of this kind with usable structured fields does print: with the built binary, a metformin order of 500 mg by mouth, once every 12 hours, with asNeededBoolean false and the text "Every twelve hours as required (qualifier value)", blocks by default with missingField and prints "Take metformin 500 mg by mouth every 12 hours." with the option. The option must therefore not be used on records of this kind unless each order's free text has been checked against its structured fields. The same check covers all 1,946 raw coded-timing orders (the 193 and four other coded phrases, `eval/coded_timing_check.py`): none prints under `--ignore-free-text` as recorded, because each blocks with missingField. Of the 1,069 orders with the phrase "Every four to six hours (qualifier value)", 455 have one milligram strength in the display name. With the drug name, a dose in milligrams, and the oral route taken from the display name, as the evaluation cases are built, all 455 print under `--ignore-free-text` as "four times a day" (376 at 1,300 mg a day and 79 at 1,600 mg a day), so the released line states one frequency for an order whose record gives a range, and one daily total. At six doses a day, which an interval of four hours would give, the 376 orders (325 mg a dose) would total 1,950 mg a day and the 79 orders (400 mg a dose) would total 2,400 mg a day. None of the 455 prints without the flag. The 221 seventy-two-hour orders and the 193 as-required orders in that form still block with missingField (`eval/data/coded_timing_check.json`). The other 614 orders with that phrase, and the 323 six-hour and 140 four-hour coded orders (1,077 orders in all), have no single milligram strength in their display name and were not tested in that form. The option should not be used on records of this kind, or on any coded-timing order, unless a clinician has checked the text. 

A drug whose label requires a meal or an empty stomach, such as metformin with meals, therefore prints without that instruction only when the record does not carry it or `--ignore-free-text` is set, so a deployment must add such warnings from clinician-written text and must not present the released text as the complete directions. Doses given as counts, such as tablets or puffs, are blocked with `missingField`, because a count gives the patient no strength to check, so a deployment must send strengths. A milligram dose also gives no number of tablets. The layer does not know the strength of the tablets the patient holds, so a patient whose tablets differ from the ordered strength cannot tell from the released text how many to take. A deployment must print the tablet count from the dispensed product or the pharmacy record, with the strength the patient holds. The evaluation used synthetic records with 11 oral drugs, normalized by our own script and without free-text fields, so it does not show how the input reader behaves on real records, and the labels were assigned by instances of the same model family that wrote the attacks. The replay relabels of F05, F03, and h1_chart_reported_by_patient were made by labelers whose identities and exposure to the version 2.1 output the repository does not record, so the adopted labels for those items carry that limitation.

**Relation to other safeguards:** The layer is not a substitute for checking orders against label limits, interaction rules, duplicate therapy, or renal and weight-based dosing; it is complementary to them. Under the default settings, an order whose dosage has text, an additional or patient instruction, or which has a note yields no dose, so every output for that patient is blocked, because an output that does not name the order is blocked for omitting it and one that names it is blocked for missing a field; real records may carry such text. In the raw synthetic bundles, 2,685 of 5,980 (44.9%) carry free text of this kind, and under the default settings no raw bundle would receive output, because no raw order states a route (Results). The option --ignore-free-text prints the structured dose and ignores that text, which may hold a limit or a hold instruction, so whether it can be ignored is a clinical decision for each kind of order. In the raw synthetic bundles, 193 active orders have as-needed wording in their free text with asNeededBoolean false, and the option would print such an order as a scheduled dose when its structured fields are usable (Limits), so the option must not be used on records of this kind without that check. A label-limit checker can show that an order is within labeled limits, which is not the same as clinically reasonable, and this layer then establishes that the model reported that order without change. The same design, in which the model selects and verified code states every safety-relevant value, applies to other quantities a model might repeat to a patient, such as laboratory thresholds or appointment times.

## Conclusion

A verified layer, with 576 lines of Lean definitions (150 of them in DoseGate/Fhir.lean, whose code tables and frequency words are trusted), 2,074 lines of machine-checked proof, and 739 non-blank lines of tested input reader and command-line code, can sit between any language model and a patient, and the properties in Table 1 show for its released text that every drug name, dose, daily total, route, and frequency that the layer fills from a slot is exactly the value of the active order that slot names, inside an instruction for that order that states its dose, route, and frequency, with an instruction for every active order by default, provided the deployment keeps drug names and extra words out of medical content and checks display names against a clinical list. The released lines are dose statements, not complete directions, so a deployment must add the weekly, as-needed, duplicate-therapy, and held-medicine checks described in Results and Limits. Table 1 names the theorem for each property. Some of those theorems, such as render_text_clean, state more than one property at once, and no single theorem states all of Table 1 together. This is fidelity to the orders, not clinical appropriateness, and it assumes that the tested input reader reads the orders correctly. The released text has not been tested for comprehension by patients, and its reading grade was estimated with a formula that the paper itself describes as unstable for texts this short. The printed units (mg, mcg, mL) are clinician abbreviations, and the printed wording was designed for a clinician who checks the text, not a patient, until a deployment decides how to spell units out. The guarantee does not depend on the model. The share of slot outputs released after rewrites, on 120 normalized synthetic test cases, ranged from 98.3% to 1.7% across three small open models. An earlier version with correct proofs failed a held-out round because its specification was too weak, and the redesigned version released 9 misstatements in 86 outputs on a second held-out round, mostly through record fields its tested input reader ignored; both results are reported, and the input reader and specification were revised in response. Version 2.1 still releases text for 16 of the 19 round-h2 requests that version 2 released and that give one ingredient under two names, for example two insulin glargine doses, so a deployment must check duplicate therapy before calling the layer. The layer does not check whether the orders it receives are correct, reasonable, or free of duplicate therapy, and it cannot recognize a brand and a generic name as one drug.
