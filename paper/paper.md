## Introduction

Medication errors are among the most common preventable causes of patient harm, and errors in the dose, frequency, and route of administration account for a large share of adverse drug events.^1,2^ Large language models are now used to explain medication regimens to patients and to draft instructions for clinicians, yet these models generate numbers by sampling tokens rather than by reading a source of truth. A recent benchmark of eight models on 230 consumer dosing questions for acetaminophen and ibuprofen reported overall accuracy between 44.8% and 62.2% and false approval of unsafe doses in 14.0% to 60.5% of cases.^3^ Placing the patient record in the prompt does not remove this failure, because the model still reproduces each number by generation, and in numeric medical data a single misplaced digit or decimal point changes a quantity by a factor of ten.^4^

Two families of safeguards are in common use. Statistical safeguards, such as a second model that judges the first or a classifier that flags unsafe text, inherit the error profile of the models they supervise and offer no guarantee on any individual output. Deterministic safeguards, such as regular expressions that compare numbers in the output with numbers in the record, are more predictable but are rarely specified precisely, and their correctness is established by testing rather than by proof. Formal verification has been applied to medical device software, most visibly to infusion pump controllers and to the entry and display of numbers on such devices,^4,5^ and proof assistants have recently been used to gate the tool calls of language model agents in other domains.^6,7^ Whether the same methods can give an end-to-end guarantee that medication statements produced by a language model agree with the patient's medication orders has not been examined.

This paper describes a small program, written and proved correct in the Lean 4 theorem prover,^8^ that sits between any language model and the reader. The model is not permitted to write a medication amount. It writes ordinary text in which the drug name, dose, route, and frequency of each medication appear only as references to fields of a Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) MedicationRequest,^9^ and the verified layer either replaces every reference with the value from the record or blocks the entire output. The guarantees are stated as theorems about the program that runs, not about the model, so they hold for every model and every output. We report the specification and its proofs, the trusted code that the proofs do not cover, and an evaluation with a four-billion-parameter open model on 240 synthetic patient records, including outputs sampled at high temperature and outputs produced under an instruction to state an unsafe dose.

## Related work

**Formal methods for medication safety:** The Generic Infusion Pump project developed formal reference models of patient-controlled analgesia pumps and derived safety requirements from them.^5^ Work on number entry has shown that the way a device parses and displays decimal quantities is itself a source of tenfold dosing errors, and has argued for designs that make such errors detectable.^4^ These efforts concern devices that administer a drug under clinician control. They do not address free text generated by a language model, and they do not tie displayed quantities to an order in the electronic health record.

**Verified guards for learned components:** Runtime enforcement of a policy by a monitor that observes and can halt a system has a long history in security,^10^ and shields that override unsafe actions of a learned controller are an established technique in safe reinforcement learning.^11^ For language models, recent systems use Lean to check each agent action against a formal policy before it executes, in financial compliance and in information-flow control for tool use.^6,7^ A related approach asks the model to synthesize a policy and verifies that policy before enforcing it.^12^ None of these systems concerns clinical content, and none constrains the numbers that appear in generated text.

**Language models and dosing:** Benchmarks report substantial error rates when language models answer dosing questions or calculate weight-based doses,^3,13^ and named entity recognition models such as Med7 can locate drug, strength, and frequency spans in clinical text.^14^ Constrained decoding restricts a model's output to a grammar,^15^ but a grammar alone does not ensure that a number agrees with the record. The present work differs from these in placing a proved program, rather than a model or an untested filter, at the point where text reaches the reader.

## Design

**Threat model and scope:** The model is treated as untrusted. It may misread the record, regress toward a typical dose, attach one drug's dose to another drug, or follow an instruction in its prompt to state a harmful amount. The patient record is treated as correct, so the layer guarantees fidelity to the record and not clinical appropriateness of the order itself; checking orders against label limits is a separate component and outside the scope of this paper. The guarantee concerns statements of amount, frequency, route, and drug identity. Statements that change meaning without an amount, such as an instruction to stop a medication, are outside the scope of the proofs and are discussed with the evaluation.

**Slot language:** The model writes plain text in which a slot of the form `{{field:id}}` stands for one field of the MedicationRequest whose identifier is `id`. The fields are the drug name, the dose, the route, the frequency, and `sig`, which expands to the complete instruction. Any other use of a brace makes the output malformed. A typical output reads "Take {{drug:m1}} {{dose:m1}} {{route:m1}} {{frequency:m1}}.", which the layer renders as "Take lisinopril 10 mg by mouth once a day." when the order specifies 10 mg orally once daily.

**The verified layer:** The function `render` receives the list of medication orders and the model's output and returns either the text to display or a reason for blocking. It proceeds in five steps. First, it rejects a record in which two orders share an identifier. Second, it parses the output into text segments and slots, and rejects malformed output. Third, it divides the output into sentences at terminal punctuation and requires that every sentence containing a dose, route, frequency, or complete instruction also contain the drug name of the same order and refer to no other order, so that one drug's dose cannot be attached to another drug's name. Fourth, it checks each text segment: the segment must be ASCII, and it must contain no dose token, defined as any word that contains a digit, any number word such as "two", "half", "twice", or "double", or any dose unit word such as "mg", "tablet", or "units". Fifth, it replaces each slot with the formatted field of the single active order that has the slot's identifier, rejects the output if the order is missing, inactive, or lacks the field, and requires a non-word character at every boundary between a text segment and a filled slot, so that the model cannot place a digit against a filled dose and turn 500 mg into 1500 mg. Only if every step succeeds does the layer return text.

**Exact quantities:** Doses are represented as exact decimals, a natural-number mantissa and a power-of-ten scale, and never as floating-point numbers. The printed form follows the rules of the Institute for Safe Medication Practices and the Joint Commission for dose designations:^16,17^ a leading zero precedes a decimal point ("0.5 mg", never ".5 mg"), no trailing zero follows one ("1 mg", never "1.0 mg"), and the unit for insulin and similar products is written "units", never "U" or "IU". Conversion between units maps each quantity to a whole number of base sub-units within one dimension (nanograms, nanolitres, thousandths of an international unit, or thousandths of a dosage form), so that a dose too fine to represent exactly is refused rather than rounded and quantities of different dimensions are never compared.
