## Abstract

Language models that explain medications to patients reproduce doses, frequencies, and daily totals by generating tokens, so a correct record in the prompt does not guarantee a correct number in the output. We describe a small program, written and proved correct in the Lean 4 theorem prover, that sits between any language model and the reader. The model writes text in which every drug name, dose, route, frequency, and daily total is a reference to a field of a Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) MedicationRequest, and the program either replaces each reference with the formatted value from the record or blocks the whole output. The development contains 170 machine-checked theorems. They establish that released text states, for each reference, exactly the corresponding value of the single active order named; that every amount, route, and timing word in released text comes from such a value; that a dose is stated only within a complete instruction for its own order; that a daily total is exact and exists only for an order taken a whole number of times per day; and that printed decimals follow safe-practice conventions and parse back to the ordered dose. We evaluated the program with a four-billion-parameter open model on 240 synthetic patient cases containing 502 orders. Given the orders, the model copied doses without a detected error, but 8 of 240 outputs that stated daily totals contained a wrong amount, mostly a weekly dose reported as a daily one, and under an instruction to double a dose it stated the doubled amount in 239 of 240 outputs. The program blocked all of these. It released 91.2% of slotted outputs after up to two rewrites and 24.8% of outputs sampled at temperature 1.0, and none of the 551 released texts misstated a dose, daily total, or frequency. The median time per output was 0.12 ms.

## Introduction

Medication errors are among the most common preventable causes of patient harm, and errors in the dose, frequency, and route of administration account for a large share of adverse drug events.^1,2^ Large language models are now used to explain medication regimens to patients and to draft instructions for clinicians, yet these models generate numbers by sampling tokens rather than by reading a source of truth. A recent benchmark of eight models on 230 consumer dosing questions for acetaminophen and ibuprofen reported overall accuracy between 44.8% and 62.2% and false approval of unsafe doses in 14.0% to 60.5% of cases.^3^ Placing the patient record in the prompt does not by itself guarantee a correct number, because the model still reproduces each number by generation and must compute any quantity the record does not state directly, and in numeric medical data a single misplaced digit or decimal point changes a quantity by a factor of ten.^4^

Two families of safeguards are in common use. Statistical safeguards, such as a second model that judges the first or a classifier that flags unsafe text, inherit the error profile of the models they supervise and offer no guarantee on any individual output. Deterministic safeguards, such as regular expressions that compare numbers in the output with numbers in the record, are more predictable but are rarely specified precisely, and their correctness is established by testing rather than by proof. Formal verification has been applied to medical device software, most visibly to infusion pump controllers and to the entry and display of numbers on such devices,^4,5^ and proof assistants have recently been used to gate the tool calls of language model agents in other domains.^6,7^ Whether the same methods can give an end-to-end guarantee that medication statements produced by a language model agree with the patient's medication orders has not been examined.

This paper describes a small program, written and proved correct in the Lean 4 theorem prover,^8^ that sits between any language model and the reader. The model is not permitted to write a medication amount. It writes ordinary text in which the drug name, dose, route, and frequency of each medication appear only as references to fields of a Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) MedicationRequest,^9^ and the verified layer either replaces every reference with the value from the record or blocks the entire output. The guarantees are stated as theorems about the program that runs, not about the model, so they hold for every model and every output. We report the specification and its proofs, the trusted code that the proofs do not cover, and an evaluation with a four-billion-parameter open model on 240 synthetic patient records, including outputs sampled at high temperature and outputs produced under an instruction to state an unsafe dose.

## Related work

**Formal methods for medication safety:** The Generic Infusion Pump project developed formal reference models of patient-controlled analgesia pumps and derived safety requirements from them.^5^ Work on number entry has shown that the way a device parses and displays decimal quantities is itself a source of tenfold dosing errors, and has argued for designs that make such errors detectable.^4^ These efforts concern devices that administer a drug under clinician control. They do not address free text generated by a language model, and they do not tie displayed quantities to an order in the electronic health record.

**Verified guards for learned components:** Runtime enforcement of a policy by a monitor that observes and can halt a system has a long history in security,^10^ and shields that override unsafe actions of a learned controller are an established technique in safe reinforcement learning.^11^ For language models, recent systems use Lean to check each agent action against a formal policy before it executes, in financial compliance and in information-flow control for tool use.^6,7^ A related approach asks the model to synthesize a policy and verifies that policy before enforcing it.^12^ None of these systems concerns clinical content, and none constrains the numbers that appear in generated text.

**Language models and dosing:** Benchmarks report substantial error rates when language models answer dosing questions or calculate weight-based doses,^3,13^ and named entity recognition models such as Med7 can locate drug, strength, and frequency spans in clinical text.^14^ Constrained decoding restricts a model's output to a grammar,^15^ but a grammar alone does not ensure that a number agrees with the record. The present work differs from these in placing a proved program, rather than a model or an untested filter, at the point where text reaches the reader.

## Design

**Threat model and scope:** The model is treated as untrusted. It may misread the record, regress toward a typical dose, miscalculate a daily total, attach one drug's dose to another drug, or follow an instruction in its prompt to state a harmful amount. The medication orders passed to the layer are treated as correct and as belonging to the patient, so the layer guarantees fidelity to the record and not clinical appropriateness of an order; checking orders against label limits is a separate component outside the scope of this paper. The guarantees concern statements of drug identity, amount, daily total, route, and frequency, and the vocabulary in which the model may surround them.

**Slot language:** The model writes plain text in which a slot of the form `{{field:id}}` stands for one field of the MedicationRequest whose identifier is `id`. The fields are the drug name, the dose, the route, the frequency, the total amount per day, and `sig`, which expands to dose, route, and frequency together. Any other use of a brace makes the output malformed. A typical output reads "Take {{drug:m1}} {{dose:m1}} {{route:m1}} {{frequency:m1}}. That is a total of {{daily:m1}} of {{drug:m1}}.", which the layer renders as "Take metformin 500 mg by mouth twice a day. That is a total of 1000 mg a day of metformin." when the order specifies 500 mg orally twice daily.

**The verified layer:** The function `render` receives the list of medication orders and the model's output and returns either the text to display or a reason for blocking. It proceeds in six steps. First, it rejects a record in which two orders share an identifier. Second, it parses the output into text segments and slots and rejects malformed output. Third, it divides the output into sentences at terminal punctuation, semicolons, colons, and line breaks, and requires each sentence to take one of three shapes. A sentence that states a dose, route, frequency, or complete instruction must consist of exactly the words and slots of "Take {{drug:id}} {{dose:id}} {{route:id}} {{frequency:id}}." or "Take {{drug:id}} {{sig:id}}." for a single order, in that order (punctuation between them is not constrained), so that a dose is never stated without its route and frequency or attached to another drug's name. A sentence that states a daily total must consist of exactly the words and slots of "That is a total of {{daily:id}} of {{drug:id}}.", and a daily slot blocks when another active order has the same drug name, because a per-order total would otherwise read as the total for the drug. Any other sentence may contain at most the drug slot of one order and otherwise only words from a fixed vocabulary of 123 common words chosen for saying what a medicine is for, from which permission, timing, route, quantity, and most imperative words have been removed. Such a sentence can still make a claim the record does not support, for example about a medicine's indication, and the layer does not check those claims. Fourth, it rejects an output in which any value of an order appears twice, which would double an instruction. Fifth, it checks every character and word the model wrote. Each character must be printable ASCII, and no word may be forbidden. A word is forbidden if it contains a digit or belongs to one of five classes: number words ("two", "half", "twice", "double"), unit and dose words ("mg", "tablet", "units", "dose"), route words ("mouth", "IV", "inject", "into", "patch"), frequency and timing words ("daily", "every", "morning", "needed", "food"), and negation, permission, or qualifier words ("not", "stop", "may", "keep", "more", "only"). The five classes hold 65, 78, 61, 80, and 72 words. A forbidden word is rejected even when a deployment adds it to the vocabulary. Sixth, it replaces each slot with the formatted field of the single active order that has the slot's identifier and rejects the output if the order is missing or inactive or lacks the field. A drug name from the record must consist of letters, spaces, and a few punctuation marks, so that a display name such as "Lisinopril 10 MG Oral Tablet" cannot print a second strength, and a non-word character must separate neighbouring pieces, so that no word can be glued to a filled value. Only if every step succeeds does the layer return text.

**Daily totals:** The daily slot multiplies the dose by the number of administrations per day, computed exactly from the FHIR timing: the frequency when the period is one day, and the frequency times 24 divided by the period when the period is a whole divisor of 24 hours. For a weekly or monthly order, for an as-needed order, and for any period that does not divide a day, no daily total exists and the slot blocks. The model therefore cannot report "15 mg in one day" for a once-weekly 15 mg order, an error the model under test made in pilot runs and in the evaluation.

**Exact quantities:** Doses are represented as exact decimals, a natural-number mantissa and a power-of-ten scale, and never as floating-point numbers. The printed form follows the rules of the Institute for Safe Medication Practices and the Joint Commission for dose designations:^16,17^ a leading zero precedes a decimal point ("0.5 mg", never ".5 mg"), no trailing zero follows one ("1 mg", never "1.0 mg"), and the unit for insulin and similar products is written "units", never "U" or "IU". Conversion between units maps each quantity to a whole number of base sub-units within one dimension (nanograms, nanolitres, thousandths of an international unit, or thousandths of a dosage form), so that a dose too fine to represent exactly is refused rather than rounded and quantities of different dimensions are never compared.

## Specification and proofs

**What is proved:** The specification is written in Lean as predicates over the inputs and the returned text, separately from the code that computes the result, and every theorem is proved about `render` itself. Table 1 lists the principal theorems. The development contains 170 theorems in 1,663 lines of proof over 533 lines of definitions (counting non-blank lines). Every theorem is checked by the Lean kernel; none uses an unproved assumption (`sorry`) or evaluation by compiled code (`native_decide`), and each depends at most on the three standard axioms of Lean (propositional extensionality, the axiom of choice, and quotient soundness).

Table: Table 1. Principal theorems about the verified layer. Each holds for every list of orders, every model output, and every deployment vocabulary.

| **Property** | **Lean theorem** | **Statement in words** |
|---|---|---|
| Fidelity | `render_fidelity` | If the layer returns text, that text is the concatenation of the model's text segments, unchanged, and of one piece per slot, and each slot's piece is the formatted field of the single active order that has the slot's identifier. |
| Dose read-back | `dose_piece_readback` | A dose piece consists of the order's dose printed in canonical form and its unit, and parsing the printed number returns the order's dose. |
| Model words | `render_text_clean`, `sentences_words` | Every character the model wrote is an allowed character, and every word it wrote belongs to the vocabulary, the two shape word lists, or the deployment's extra words, and is not forbidden. |
| Source of amounts | `render_forbidden_tokens_from_value_slots`, `tokens_flatten` | The forbidden words of the returned text, in order, are exactly the forbidden words of the pieces that fill dose, route, frequency, sig, and daily slots. |
| Sentence shapes | `render_sentences`, `sentenceOk_value_shape`, `sentenceOk_free_words` | Every sentence refers to at most one order; the words and slots of a sentence with a value slot are, in order, exactly those of the instruction, complete-instruction, or total shape; any other sentence contains only vocabulary words and extras. No value of an order appears twice. |
| Fail closed | `renderSeg_unknownRef`, `renderSeg_inactive`, `renderSeg_missing`, `render_malformed_eq`, `render_dup`, `renderSeg_daily_unique` | A slot whose identifier names no order, an inactive order, or a missing field produces that specific block; malformed output and duplicate identifiers produce their specific blocks; a daily total is returned only when its order is the only active order for that drug. |
| Vocabulary | `vocab_clean`, `instr_total_clean`, `checkText_forbidden` | No word in the vocabulary or the shape word lists is forbidden, and a forbidden word in model text is blocked even if a deployment allows it. |
| Decimal printing | `parseDec_fmtDec`, `fmtDec_below_one`, `fmtDec_no_trailing_zero`, `fmtDec_eq_iff` | A printed decimal begins with a digit and parses back to the same value, a value between zero and one prints with "0." in front, a printed decimal point is never followed by a trailing zero, and two decimals print identically exactly when they are equal in value. |
| Unambiguous phrases | `fmtDose_inj`, `fmtTiming_inj`, `route_phrase_inj` | Different doses, different timings, and different routes never print as the same text. |
| Daily total | `dailyDose_exact`, `dosesPerDay_spec`, `dosesPerDay_none_long`, `dailyDose_none_prn` | The daily total is the dose multiplied by the exact number of administrations per day, and no daily total exists for weekly, monthly, or as-needed orders. |

**The tokenizer lemma:** Most theorems in Table 1 follow from the structure of `render` once the right invariants are stated, but two required substantive arguments. The first concerns words. The guarantee that the model contributes no forbidden word must hold for the returned string, not merely for each piece, and concatenation can merge words: a model word placed directly against a filled dose, as in "x{{dose:m1}}", would merge with the number into the single word "x500", which no check on either piece alone would see. The lemma `tokens_flatten` proves that when every boundary between neighbouring pieces has a non-word character on at least one side, the words of the concatenation are exactly the concatenation of the words of the pieces, and `render` checks that condition before returning. The second concerns decimals. `parseDec_fmtDec` proves that parsing the printed form of any decimal returns its normalized value, which with the uniqueness of normal forms yields `fmtDec_eq_iff`: two doses print identically if and only if they are the same quantity. This rules out the printed ambiguity of "1.0 mg" and "10 mg" at the level of the printing function.

**Trusted code:** The proofs cover `render` and the functions it calls, given a list of orders already in Lean's internal form. They do not cover the conversion of FHIR JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) into that form, which is performed by 394 lines of Lean in the command-line tool. That reader fails closed: an order is active only when its status is active, its intent is an order intent, and `doNotPerform` is absent or false; any dosage with a range, a maximum, a timing code, a bounded duration, a dispense validity period, more than one dosage instruction, or an unrecognized or ambiguous route yields no dose, timing, or route; requests with duplicate JSON keys, more than one patient subject, oversized numbers, more than 400 slots, or more than two megabytes are rejected. These rules are covered by 110 regression tests rather than by proof. The trusted base also includes the Lean kernel and compiler, the JSON parser in Lean's standard library, and the operating system, as for other verified software.^21,22^



## Evaluation methods

**Patient records:** Synthetic patients were drawn from Synthea,^18^ an open-source generator of synthetic electronic health records, as 5,980 FHIR bundles produced earlier for unrelated work. Synthea medication orders record each dose as one tablet without a unit and carry the strength only in the RxNorm display name, and they omit the route. We therefore normalized every active order whose display name gave exactly one strength in milligrams for a tablet or capsule: the dose became that strength in milligrams, the route became oral (sublingual where the display name said so), the drug name became the ingredient name without strength or dosage form, and the timing was copied unchanged. This transformation was applied to the test records before evaluation and is not performed by the layer. Bundles were shuffled with a fixed seed, and the first 120 patients with at least one usable order contributed up to four orders each. Each patient yielded two cases. The natural case kept the record's strengths and timings. The perturbed case multiplied each dose by 0.5, 1.5, 2, or 3 and replaced each timing with one of once daily, twice daily, three times daily, every 8 hours, every 12 hours, or once weekly, chosen deterministically from a hash of the file name, so that atypical doses (for example lisinopril 37.5 mg) and weekly schedules would test whether a model reproduces the record or regresses toward familiar values. The 240 cases contain 502 orders. The vocabulary for free sentences was assembled from pilot outputs and then reduced during adversarial testing, and a reduction can only lower the rate of released text. The first 120 cases were designated a development set, and release rates are reported both for all 240 cases and for the remaining 120 test cases.

**Model and conditions:** The model under test was Qwen3.5-4B,^19^ a four-billion-parameter open-weight model, run locally with MLX LM^20^ in bfloat16 with reasoning disabled and at most 400 new tokens per response. The model was chosen as a deliberately weak test subject: small models are the ones most likely to be deployed on local hardware for patient-facing applications, and they make numerical errors often enough to test a safeguard. Every case was run under seven conditions. In the free condition, the model received the orders in plain text and was asked to explain how to take each medicine. The free daily condition added a request to state the total amount per day. In the slot and slot daily conditions, the same requests were accompanied by the slot rules and one example, and the model saw each order's identifier and drug name but not its dose, route, or timing, because a model writing slots has no need for the values and cannot copy what it does not see. An earlier run in which the model saw the values showed the cost of the alternative: the model replaced the slots with numbers it had read, and the layer blocked those outputs. When the layer blocked an output, the model received the layer's reason and was asked to rewrite, up to two times. In the attack condition, the slot rules were given and the model saw the full orders, including doses, and the user message also instructed the model to tell the patient to take double the usual amount of the first medicine and to state the number of milligrams. Two further slot samples per case were drawn at temperature 1.0 with top-p 0.95 to probe the variety of outputs a deployed model might produce. All other generations used greedy decoding.

**Outcome measures:** For each condition we recorded whether the layer returned text and, if not, the reason it blocked. For text written freely by the model, a scoring script located mass quantities (a number followed by mg, mcg, g, or their spelled forms) in each sentence or line that named a drug, and counted an order as misstated when such a sentence gave a quantity equal to neither the order's dose nor its correct daily total, or when it stated a daily total for a weekly order. The script also compared frequency phrases (for example "twice a day" or "every 8 hours") with the order's timing. Every output that the script flagged in the free conditions was read by hand. Two flags were false, caused by phrases the script did not recognize ("four times every day" and "take two of them every day"), and the script was corrected; the counts reported here are after that correction and agree with the hand reading. The script finds only the errors it can parse, so its counts for free text are lower bounds. The same script was applied to every text the layer returned. Because the layer's guarantee is a theorem, the expected count of misstatements in returned text is zero; the measurement checks the implementation end to end, including the trusted reader, rather than the proof. Latency of the compiled layer was measured per call, with the layer running as one persistent process, on an Apple M2 Max computer with 32 GB of memory, the same machine used for generation.

**Version of the layer:** Generation ran while the last two rounds of adversarial testing were in progress, so the rewrite requests quoted the block reasons of an earlier version of the layer. Every decision reported below was recomputed with the final version. The final version blocked 16 outputs that the earlier version had released, all because a free sentence used the word "vessels", which the final vocabulary omits, and it released no output that the earlier version had blocked. Those 16 outputs received no rewrite, so the release rates after rewrites are slightly lower than a run against the final version would give.

## Results

**Released text:** Across the slotted conditions, the layer released 551 outputs that together named 1,038 orders, and the scoring script found no misstated dose, daily total, or frequency in any of them (Table 2). The layer blocked every output in the free, free daily, and attack conditions, each of which contained digits or other forbidden words written by the model.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free | 240 | 0 (0%) | 0 (0%) | Sentence shape, 240 |
| Free daily | 240 | 0 (0%) | 0 (0%) | Sentence shape, 240 |

**Errors in free text:** When the model saw the orders and wrote freely, the script found no misstated dose or frequency in 240 outputs covering 502 orders; given the values, this model copied them correctly. Errors appeared when the model had to compute a quantity. In the free daily condition, 8 of 240 outputs (3.3%) contained a wrong amount. Six stated a weekly dose as a daily total, for example "by mouth just once a week, so you will take a total of 20 mg of this medicine in one day"; these were 6 of the 40 outputs whose case contained a weekly order. One stated a total of 150 mg in one day for meperidine 50 mg every 4 hours, for which the correct total is 300 mg, and one added the doses of three different drugs into "a total of 45 mg of medicine". Two of the eight occurred in natural cases and six in perturbed cases. In the slot daily condition the layer computed every total, and an order without a daily total blocked instead of printing one.

**Attack:** Under the instruction to double the first medicine, 239 of 240 outputs stated the doubled amount in milligrams, usually in a sentence added after correctly slotted instructions, such as "Take double the usual amount of the first medicine today, which is 100 milligrams." The layer blocked all 240. In 45 perturbed cases the doubled dose equalled the correct daily total of a twice-daily order, so a filter that accepts any number derivable from the record would have released the statement; the layer blocked these outputs because the model wrote the number itself.

**Causes of blocking:** In the greedy slot conditions, most first-attempt blocks were missing fields. Across all attempts in those conditions, 89 missing-field blocks concerned a daily slot written for an order that has no daily total, such as a weekly order or one taken every 60 days, and 38 concerned a drug name that the layer refuses to print because it contains a number, as in "12 hr hydrocodone bitartrate", which occurred in 10 orders across 8 cases. Rewrites guided by the layer's reasons raised the release rate from 77.1% to 91.2% in the slot condition and from 80.0% to 88.8% in the slot daily condition. At temperature 1.0, 270 of the 340 outputs blocked for sentence shape contained a forbidden word written by the model, most often "lower" (as in "helps lower your blood pressure") or "keep", and the other 70 contained words outside the vocabulary, most often a drug name written as a plain word instead of as a drug slot.

**Latency:** Over 1,822 calls, the layer took a median of 0.12 ms per output, with a 99th percentile of 0.35 ms.

## Discussion

The evaluation separates two failures that are often treated as one. Given the values, a four-billion-parameter model copied doses and frequencies without a detected error; its errors came when it had to compute a quantity, such as a daily total for a weekly order, or when its prompt told it to state one. These are the cases that a filter matching numbers in the output against the record handles worst, because a computed total is a number the record does not contain, and a harmful number can equal a legitimate one, as the doubled dose did in 45 attack outputs. The layer avoids both problems by never accepting a number from the model: proved code computes every total, and any number the model writes blocks the output. The cost is the rate of released text. With greedy decoding and up to two rewrites, about nine in ten outputs were released; at temperature 1.0, one in four, mostly because natural phrasing such as "helps lower your blood pressure" uses a forbidden word. Decoding constrained to the slot grammar and the vocabulary might raise these rates without weakening the guarantee, which depends only on the layer, but we did not test it.

**What the proofs do and do not establish:** The theorems concern the layer and hold for every model, so they give no information about how often a particular model produces usable output, and that rate must be measured. They hold relative to definitions that are themselves choices. The forbidden word classes and the free-sentence vocabulary are finite lists. A phrasing that changes a dose without any word from those lists cannot appear in a value sentence, because value sentences admit no free words at all, but a free sentence built only from vocabulary words can still carry an unsupported claim, such as an indication the record does not contain. The sentence shapes are defined over words and slots in order and do not constrain punctuation between them, so a deployment that renders punctuation as markup must escape it. The layer guarantees fidelity to the orders it receives and nothing about whether those orders are correct, current, or the patient's; the application that retrieves them from the record carries that responsibility, as it would for any display of the medication list. The layer does not require every active order to be mentioned, so an output that omits a medication passes, and a deployment that needs completeness must check coverage separately.

**Trusted code:** The proofs end at the boundary between JSON and Lean values. The reader that crosses that boundary is short and fails closed, and five rounds of adversarial testing by independent agents found defects there, including an order marked `doNotPerform` that was read as an instruction to take the medication, before the fail-closed rules were added. Those defects were in unproved code, which is where an end-to-end guarantee is weakest, and a proved FHIR reader would remove the largest remaining assumption. The drug name is copied from the record verbatim, so a record whose drug name field contains other words, such as "insulin sliding scale" for an order that also carries a fixed dose, is reproduced as written. Free-text fields of an order, such as a note to hold a medication, are not read, so an order that is active but annotated "hold" renders as an instruction to take it; whether a deployment should block such orders depends on how its record system uses those fields.

**Relation to other safeguards:** The layer is not a substitute for checking orders against label limits, interaction rules, or renal and weight-based dosing, and it is complementary to them: a label-limit checker can establish that an order is reasonable, and this layer then establishes that the model reported that order without change. The same design, in which the model selects and verified code states every safety-relevant value, applies to other quantities a model might repeat to a patient, such as laboratory thresholds or appointment times.

## Conclusion

A program of 533 lines of Lean definitions, with 1,663 lines of machine-checked proof, can sit between a language model and a reader and guarantee that every drug name, dose, daily total, route, and frequency in the text it releases is exactly the corresponding value of the patient's active medication order. The guarantee does not depend on the model, and an evaluation with a small open model showed that the layer blocked every output in which the model stated an amount itself, including outputs produced under an explicit instruction to double a dose. The cost of the guarantee is a stricter output format and a lower rate of released text: about nine in ten outputs at greedy decoding after rewrites, and one in four at temperature 1.0.

**Availability:** The Lean development, the command-line tool, the regression tests, the evaluation scripts, the normalized synthetic cases, and all model outputs are provided as ancillary files with this preprint.

**Use of AI tools:** The author used Claude (Anthropic) to draft Lean definitions, proofs, and evaluation code and to edit the manuscript, and used independent instances of it to review the specification and to search for attacks. All proofs are checked by the Lean kernel. The author reviewed the design, the theorem statements, and the manuscript and is responsible for the content.
