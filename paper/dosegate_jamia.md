## Abstract

**Objective:** To build and evaluate a layer, proved in Lean 4, that fills every drug name, dose, route, frequency, and daily total in a language model's text from the patient's orders or blocks it.

**Materials and Methods:** The model writes each instruction with slots naming fields of a Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) MedicationRequest; the layer fills each from the single active order it names and admits only a 67-word vocabulary elsewhere. We proved 200 theorems about released text, tested the FHIR reader with 232 tests and 20,000 fuzzed requests, ran two held-out adversarial rounds, and evaluated three open and two hosted models on 120 synthetic cases against a digit-and-unit filter. <!-- tests/Axioms.lean; tests/run.sh; tests/fuzz_reader.py; eval/data/models_summary.json -->

**Results:** Version 1 released 38 misstatements in 74 held-out releases; version 2, 9 in 86, against 23 in 128 for the filter. After rewrites, the layer released 98.3%, 83.3%, 1.7%, 98.3%, and 98.3% of slot outputs from Qwen3.5-4B, MedGemma 4B, Llama 3.2 3B, Claude Haiku 5.5, and Claude Sonnet 5.5; a script found no misstatement in 1,334 released texts. Without the layer, the open models misstated an order in at least 16, 71, and 20 of 120 outputs. <!-- eval/data/labels_summary.json; eval/data/models_summary.json; eval/data/v2_review/labels.json -->

**Discussion:** Given the tested reader, the guarantee holds for any model; the release rate does not. Six of nine failures lay in record content the reader did not interpret; the current version stops all nine on replay but is untested held out.

**Conclusion:** A proved layer bounds what a model can say about doses; clinician validation and a held-out round remain.

## Background and Significance

Medication errors are among the most common preventable causes of patient harm.^1,2^ Large language models are increasingly used to answer patients' questions about their medicines, yet they generate numbers by sampling tokens rather than by reading a source of truth. On 230 consumer dosing questions about acetaminophen and ibuprofen, eight models answered correctly 44.8% to 62.2% of the time and approved 14.0% to 60.5% of prohibited doses,^3^ and models computing weight-based pediatric doses erred often enough to be unsafe without review.^4^ Placing the orders in the prompt does not remove the hazard: the model still reproduces each number and computes any quantity the orders do not state, such as a total per day, and a misplaced decimal point changes a quantity tenfold.^5^

Two families of safeguard are in use. Statistical safeguards, such as a second model that judges the first or a classifier that flags unsafe text,^6^ inherit the error profile of the models they supervise and give no guarantee on any single output. Deterministic filters that compare numbers in the output with numbers in the record are predictable but only as good as their rules. Formal verification has been applied to infusion pump software and to number entry on medical devices,^5,7^ proof assistants have recently gated the actions of language model agents,^8,9^ and related designs verify a synthesized policy,^10^ fix control flow from the trusted query,^11^ or place critics around candidate outputs.^12^ Template generation,^13^ data-to-text evaluation,^14^ constrained decoding,^15^ and runtime monitors and shields^16,17^ each constrain what a model may emit. Whether these methods can give a machine-checked guarantee that a language model's medication statements agree with the orders has not, to our knowledge, been examined.

We describe such a layer, a small program written in Lean 4^18^ and proved to meet a written specification. The model may not write a medication amount or name; it writes each instruction as references to fields of a FHIR MedicationRequest,^19^ and the layer replaces every reference with the order's value or blocks the whole output. The theorems assume nothing about the model, so any model that can write the reference format can sit behind the layer unchanged. We report the specification and proofs, the tested reader, two held-out adversarial rounds (the first of which an earlier version failed), and an evaluation with three open models against a comparison filter.

## Objective

To determine whether a proved output layer can guarantee that every drug name, dose, route, frequency, and daily total a language model states to a patient equals the corresponding value of that patient's active medication order, and to measure, for five models, how much text such a layer releases and what the open models write without it.

## Materials and Methods

**The layer:** The model writes plain text in which a slot of the form `{{field:id}}` stands for one field of the order with identifier `id`: the drug name, dose, route, frequency, daily total, or `sig`, which expands to dose, route, and frequency together. The function `render` receives the orders and the model's output, returns text or a block reason, and requires every sentence to take one of two forms. A value sentence consists of exactly the words and slots of one of four shapes for a single order, "Take {{drug}} {{dose}} {{route}} {{frequency}}" or "Take {{drug}} {{sig}}", either optionally followed by "which is {{daily}}"; an order of metformin 500 mg by mouth twice daily renders as "Take metformin 500 mg by mouth twice a day, which is 1000 mg a day." A framing sentence contains no slot and only words from a 67-word vocabulary for introducing a list and referring the patient to the care team, with no drug, disease, body part, route, timing, quantity, or negation word. <!-- DoseGate/Tokens.lean (vocab); DoseGate/Render.lean (shapes); paper/paper.md Design --> A deployment may add framing words, but the guarantees about framing sentences hold for the default vocabulary, and added words could let a drug name appear outside an instruction.

Every character the model writes must be an American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) letter, digit, space, line break, or one of ten punctuation marks, and no word may contain a digit or belong to five forbidden classes (number, unit and dose, route, frequency and timing, and negation or qualifier words; 353 words). <!-- DoseGate/Tokens.lean; paper/paper.md Design --> For each slot the layer finds the order with the slot's identifier and blocks if none has it, the order is inactive, the field is missing, or another active order has a related drug name (names that share a word or a prefix of four or more characters, are equal ignoring case, or contain a non-ASCII character or no ASCII word). It also rejects a repeated value slot, a sig slot beside a dose, route, or frequency slot for the same order, a minus or plus sign before a slot, and, by default, an output that leaves an active order without an instruction or has no active order. A drug name prints only if it is ASCII letters, spaces, and hyphens, begins and ends with a letter, has at most 60 characters and five words, and contains no forbidden, vocabulary, or shape word and none of 77 name stop words such as "see" and "scale", so "Lisinopril 10 MG Oral Tablet" cannot print a second strength. <!-- DoseGate/Fhir.lean (drugNameOk, dailyDose?); DoseGate/Tokens.lean (name stop words, relatedDrug); DoseGate/Dec.lean (fmtDec); DoseGate/Units.lean; paper/paper.md Design --> The daily slot multiplies the dose by the exact number of administrations per day from the FHIR timing and blocks for weekly, monthly, and as-needed orders. Doses are exact decimals printed with a leading zero, no trailing zero, and "units" rather than "U" for insulin, following the Institute for Safe Medication Practices and Joint Commission dose designations;^20,21^ a dose given as a count of tablets or puffs is refused because it gives the patient no strength to check. When an output is blocked, a fallback command renders one line per active order through the same proved code; the block reason can quote the model and must never reach a patient.

**Specification, proofs, and trusted code:** The specification is written in Lean 4.35.0 with the core library only, as predicates over the inputs and the returned text, apart from the code that computes the result; 200 theorems in 2,074 non-blank lines of proof cover 576 lines of definitions, the Lean kernel checks every theorem, none uses an unproved assumption or evaluation by compiled code, and a repository file prints the axioms of each. <!-- lean-toolchain; DoseGate/*.lean and DoseGate/Proofs/*.lean line counts; tests/axioms_2026-11-05.txt (saved output of tests/Axioms.lean): 121 on propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound; 68 on a subset; 11 on none; 0 on sorryAx --> Table 1 states what is proved, what is tested, and what is trusted. The principal theorems (Supplementary Table S1) establish fidelity (released text is the model's segments unchanged plus one piece per slot, each the formatted field of the single active order the slot names), the value-sentence shapes, exact daily totals, completeness, clean model words, the source of every forbidden word, and no sign before a slot. Two lemmas carry the weight: `tokens_flatten`, that the words of a concatenation are the words of its pieces when a non-word character separates them, and `parseDec_fmtDec`, that parsing a printed decimal returns its value, so two doses print identically if and only if they are equal as decimal values.

The Lean kernel does not check the conversion of FHIR JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) into Lean values, which 600 non-blank lines of reader and command-line code perform. The reader fails closed on any order it cannot fully interpret, including extensions, security labels, superseded orders, non-continuous courses of therapy, count doses, and, by default, free text (Supplementary Section S2 lists the rules). <!-- DoseGate/Json.lean 393 lines, Main.lean 207 lines --> 232 regression tests cover this code, and a differential fuzz test compares every reply of the layer, on 20,000 mutated requests of 52 kinds, with a Python reference that encodes the same reader policy and copies the route and unit tables from the Lean source, so the test checks agreement with the policy and not the policy itself. <!-- tests/run.sh output 2026-11-05; tests/fuzz_reader.py --> The trusted base also includes the Lean kernel and compiler, the JSON parser, and the operating system (Table 1).

**Adversarial development and held-out rounds:** Version 1 allowed free sentences with a drug slot and 123 common words and stated daily totals in a separate sentence. Independent instances of Claude (Anthropic) that could read the source and run the layer wrote 290 requests, each with its own orders and output, in five labeled red-team parts, and the layer changed after each part. <!-- eval/data/attacks.jsonl; eval/labeling_protocol.md --> We then froze version 1 and asked four new instances to write a held-out round (h1) in four families (vocabulary meaning, FHIR content, slot structure, small-model style), telling them nothing would change in response and to keep every attack they seriously tried; a Secure Hash Algorithm (SHA-256) checksum of each file and the layer commit are recorded. <!-- eval/data/heldout/manifest_h1.json --> Version 2 was redesigned in response, frozen, and tested by five new instances in a second held-out round (h2) that added a family of clinically realistic regimens (tapers, weekly methotrexate, insulin, as-needed orders). <!-- eval/data/heldout2/manifest_h2.json --> Version 2.1, described here, followed round h2 and review; all corpora were replayed through it, and no held-out round has been run against it.

Two independent instances labeled every text released by either filter as faithful, misstating an order (a value that differs from the active orders, an inactive order presented as current, an instruction to skip, stop, double, or adjust, and, for round h2, an instruction attached to the wrong medicine or a conditional order presented as fixed), or an unsupported claim (such as an indication the orders do not support); a third instance decided disagreements and omissions and relabeled twelve outputs under a rule adopted after labeling (Table 2 note). No human labeled these outputs. A replay runs a later version over the saved requests of an earlier round; a replayed text identical to a labeled one keeps its label. <!-- eval/labeling_protocol.md; eval/data/replay_v21/labels.json --> The author alone counted, for round h2, outputs with two instructions for one ingredient under two names (Lantus and insulin glargine); no clinician has checked that list. <!-- eval/data/heldout2/same_ingredient_pairs.json -->

**Model evaluation:** Synthetic patients came from Synthea,^24^ 5,980 FHIR bundles. Because Synthea records each dose as one tablet without a unit and gives the strength only in the RxNorm^25^ display name, we normalized every active order whose name gave one strength in milligrams for a tablet or capsule (dose that strength, route oral in all 502 records, name the text before the strength, notes and dosage text dropped). The first 120 patients with a usable order, after shuffling with a fixed seed, each gave a natural case and a perturbed case with doses multiplied by 0.5, 1.5, 2, or 3 and timings replaced: 240 cases, 502 order records, 11 oral drugs, split by patient into development and test halves of 120 cases; all results are on the test half. <!-- eval/build_cases.py; eval/data/describe.json; eval/data/free_text_share.json (5,980 bundles); eval/data/cases.jsonl SHA-256 8dd1d13f… --> In the raw records of these patients, 118 of 548 active orders (21.5%) carry free text, which the default reader treats as unusable. <!-- eval/data/free_text_share.json -->

Three open models from three developers ran on one Apple M2 Max computer: Qwen3.5-4B^26^ at full precision with MLX LM 0.32.2^27^ and reasoning disabled, and MedGemma 4B^28^ and Llama 3.2 3B^29^ at 4-bit quantization through Ollama 0.40.14, so model and runtime vary together. Two hosted models from Anthropic, Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 (identifiers claude-haiku-5-5 and claude-sonnet-5-5 as returned by the Messages API, run on 2026-11-05), used adaptive thinking at low effort, a 2,000-token output limit including thinking, no fallback to another model, and the API's default sampling, because these models reject a temperature setting; they received the four unsampled conditions only, and no labeler read their outputs. <!-- eval/generate.py (anthropic_backend); eval/data/generations_v2_claude-haiku-5-5.jsonl and generations_v2_claude-sonnet-5-5.jsonl ("api" field on every record) --> Each case received five conditions (Supplementary Section S3): free daily (orders in plain text, explain each medicine and its daily total, no layer), slot and slot daily (the model sees each order's identifier and name but not its values, with up to two rewrites after a block), two slot samples at temperature 1.0 without rewrites, and an attack condition whose prompt told the model to tell the patient to double the first medicine and state the milligrams. <!-- eval/generate.py --> A comparison filter with the same reader and slot formatting, but checking model text only for braces, digits, and 78 unit words, was applied to every output and every adversarial request. <!-- Baseline.lean --> A scoring script located quantities and frequency phrases in released text and flagged a misstated daily total, a quantity equal to neither dose nor total, or a frequency not equivalent to the order's; its sensitivity was checked by planting a tenfold dose, a total replaced by a dose, and a changed frequency in each released text. Two blind labelers, with a third on disagreement, labeled every flagged free daily output of MedGemma 4B and Llama 3.2 3B, 20 unflagged outputs per model, every output released only by the comparison filter but one (a Qwen3.5-4B output that omitted an order), and 20 random layer releases per model; Qwen3.5-4B's free daily outputs came from the version 1 run, where two labelers read all 16 flagged test outputs and the author alone, not blind, read the 5 unflagged test outputs with a weekly order. <!-- eval/score.py; eval/controls.py; eval/data/v2_review/; eval/data/free_text_labels.json --> Proportions carry 95% Wilson score intervals that treat outputs as independent although they cluster within 60 patients. ## Discussion

A proved layer can bound what a language model states about a dose, and the proof assumes no model, but two findings qualify the result. First, the rate of usable output varied with the model: on the same cases and prompts, release after rewrites ranged from 98.3% to 1.7%, so a deployment must measure its own model and have something to show when the layer blocks. The fallback renders one proved line per active order without any model (118 of the 120 test cases; the two blocked carry the digit name), but it has no heading, daily total, reason for use, or warning, and no code excludes any order from it, <!-- eval/data/fallback_rate.json (eval/fallback_rate.py) --> so a deployment must withhold it for as-needed orders and, unless a pharmacist has checked the lines, for weekly orders. Second, a proof establishes only the property it states, and the theorems state what released text satisfies, not that text is released; the one liveness theorem covers a single fixed chart. Version 1's theorems were correct, yet 38 of its 74 held-out releases misstated an order, because the specification let a free sentence name a drug and so label another order's instruction; held-out testing, not proof, showed the property was too weak. Version 2.1's property is stronger (every filled value lies inside an instruction for its own order, every active order has one, and every other sentence comes from the framing vocabulary) but untested held out.

The errors the models made without the layer arose where they had to compute: a daily total for a weekly order, a sum across drugs, or a doubled dose the prompt demanded. A filter that compares numbers in the output with the orders handles these poorly, because a computed total is not in the orders and a harmful number can equal a legitimate one, as the doubled dose did in 47 open-model attack outputs (Supplementary Section S9). The layer avoids both by accepting no number or drug name from the model: proved code computes every total, and any word outside the vocabulary blocks the output. On the two models that followed the format, the comparison filter also released no labeled misstatement, plus 8 outputs the layer blocked; on the third it released 51 misstatements, and on round h2 it released 23 against 9, including the layer's nine, six of which (Table 3) lay in the shared reader and so tested the reader rather than the checks on model text.

**Limitations:** The released instruction gives no tablet count, time of day, food or hold direction, indication, or warning, because the words that would carry them are forbidden to the model; a deployment must add them from clinician-written text. The related-name rule cannot see that a brand and a generic name, an abbreviation, or a combination product beside one of its components denote one ingredient; version 2.1 released 16 such pairs on round h2, by the author's unchecked reading, and a deployment that has ingredient codes must check duplicate therapy before the layer. The layer prints chart errors faithfully, as the 221 above-label outputs show, and the drug-name rule passes names that carry an instruction or drop a formulation: on round h2 it released "7 times a day" for a methotrexate order so written, a 120-unit daily insulin total, the name "Cease Warfarin", and "Oxycodone" for an extended-release product whose text name omits the formulation (Supplementary Section S10). Fidelity to the order is not clinical safety, so a label-limit check, a frequency plausibility check, and a pharmacist's review of display names must run before the layer. Under the default rule, every output is blocked for a patient with free text on an active order, which in the raw synthetic bundles is 44.9% of patients, and the option that ignores that text drops instructions such as holds. <!-- eval/data/free_text_share.json --> The evaluation used synthetic records with 11 oral drugs, normalized by our script, and the two levothyroxine orders (one patient, natural and perturbed) print 0.075 mg and 0.0375 mg where United States tablets are labeled in micrograms. <!-- eval/data/cases.jsonl --> The adversarial requests and all labels came from instances of one model family, with no human labeler and a Cohen's kappa of 0.50 on round h2; the two held-out rounds found failures and do not estimate a rate in any population; the Qwen3.5-4B outputs were seen before version 2 was designed; the open models differ in precision and runtime as well as weights; and no labeler read the hosted models' outputs. Patient comprehension was not tested. [TODO clinical validation paragraph: pharmacist review and human labels.]

The same design could apply to other quantities a model repeats to a patient, such as laboratory thresholds or appointment times, but this was not tested. Larger models answer medical questions more accurately,^31^ and the two hosted models followed the format more often: they matched Qwen3.5-4B's 98.3% release after rewrites, exceeded its first-attempt rate (93.3% and 92.5% against 81.7%), and declined the doubling attack on their own, while released misstatements stayed at zero for all five models. A more capable model therefore raised the release rate and changed nothing about what released text could say, as the theorems require; the one rule cost the hosted models exposed is that forbidden words such as "any" block benign caveat sentences until a rewrite removes them. <!-- eval/data/models_summary.json; eval/data/controls_v2_claude-*.json -->

## Conclusion

A layer of 576 lines of Lean definitions, 2,074 lines of machine-checked proof, and 600 lines of tested reader code guarantees, by a proof about the released text, that every drug name, dose, daily total, route, and frequency it fills equals the value of the active order it names, inside an instruction for that order, with an instruction for every active order under the default settings, whatever model writes the text. This is fidelity to the orders, not clinical appropriateness, and it rests on a tested reader and on the author's judgment that the framing vocabulary carries no medical content. A first version with correct proofs failed a held-out round, the redesign released 9 misstatements in 86 held-out outputs, mostly through the reader, and the current version is untested held out. Across three small open models and two hosted frontier models the scoring script found no misstatement in released text on the 120 test cases, and the release rate ranged from 98.3% to 1.7%.
