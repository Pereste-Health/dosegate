## Abstract

**Objective:** To test whether a layer verified in Lean 4 keeps slot-filled doses, frequencies, and daily totals equal to orders.

**Materials and Methods:** The model writes slots for Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) MedicationRequest fields; the layer fills each from its order or blocks the output. Framing words holding no drug name are judged, not proved. We proved 201 theorems, tested the reader, ran adversarial rounds, and evaluated five models on 120 normalized synthetic test cases. <!-- tests/Axioms.lean; tests/run.sh; tests/fuzz_reader.py; eval/data/models_summary.json; paper/paper.md Abstract and Development -->

**Results:** On 138 adversarial requests, version 2 released 9 misstatements in 86 texts, a digit-and-unit comparison filter 23 in 128. Version 2.1 blocks all 9 on replay; 16 of its 45 released texts give one ingredient under two names, a possible double dose. After rewrites it released 98.3% of slot outputs from Qwen3.5-4B, Claude Haiku 5.5, and Claude Sonnet 5.5, 83.3% from MedGemma 4B, and 1.7% from Llama 3.2 3B. A lenient script found no misstated dose, total, or frequency in 1,334 released texts; no clinician reviewed any. Without the layer, Qwen3.5-4B misstated an order in at least 16 of 120 outputs, MedGemma 4B in 71, and Llama 3.2 3B in 20. <!-- eval/data/labels_summary.json; eval/data/models_summary.json; eval/data/replay_v21/h2_results.jsonl; eval/data/v2_review/labels.json; paper/paper.md Abstract and Results -->

**Discussion:** Given the tested, unproved reader, the guarantee holds for any model; the release rate does not. Weekly, monthly, as-needed, conditional, and taper lines need pharmacist review, and wording is untested. <!-- paper/paper.md Results; eval/data/heldout2/labels_h2.json -->

**Conclusion:** A verified layer makes each filled dose and frequency match its order, not clinical appropriateness. Released lines are dose statements, not complete directions; clinician validation and a held-out round remain. <!-- paper/paper.md Abstract and Conclusion -->

## Background and Significance

Medication errors are among the most common preventable causes of patient harm.^1,2^ Large language models are increasingly used to answer patients' questions about their medicines, yet they generate numbers by sampling tokens rather than by reading a source of truth. On 230 consumer dosing questions about acetaminophen and ibuprofen, eight models answered correctly 44.8% to 62.2% of the time and approved 14.0% to 60.5% of prohibited doses,^3^ and models calculating weight-based pediatric doses made substantial errors.^4^ Placing the orders in the prompt does not remove the hazard: the model still reproduces each number and computes any quantity the orders do not state, such as a total per day, and a misplaced decimal point changes a quantity tenfold.^5^ <!-- paper/paper.md Introduction (DoseBench is reference 3 there) -->

Two families of safeguard are in use. Statistical safeguards, such as a second model that judges the first or a classifier that flags unsafe text,^6^ inherit the models' error profile and give no guarantee on any single output. Deterministic filters that compare output numbers with the record are predictable but only as good as their rules. Formal verification has been used in medicine before, to prove properties of the software in infusion pumps, which deliver intravenous drugs at set rates, and of the keypads on which clinicians enter doses,^5,7^ and proof assistants have recently gated the actions of language model agents.^8,9^ Related designs verify a synthesized policy,^10^ fix control flow from the trusted query,^11^ or place critics around candidate outputs.^12^ Template generation,^13^ data-to-text evaluation,^14^ constrained decoding,^15^ and runtime monitors and shields^16,17^ each constrain what a model may emit. Whether these methods can give a machine-checked guarantee that a language model's medication statements agree with the orders has not, to our knowledge, been examined.

We describe such a layer: a small program written in Lean 4^18^ with machine-checked proofs that each drug name, dose, route, frequency, and daily total in its released text equals the matching field of the active order it names; those proved statements are its specification (Supplementary Table S1). The model writes each instruction as references to fields of a FHIR MedicationRequest,^19^ and the layer replaces every reference with the order's value or blocks the whole output (Figure 1). The model cannot state an amount or a drug name itself: the layer blocks any output containing a digit, a number, unit, route, or frequency word, or any word outside a 67-word default vocabulary and four sentence shapes, so amounts reach the patient only through references the layer fills. The theorems assume nothing about the model, so any model that writes the reference format can sit behind the layer unchanged. We report the specification, the tested reader, the adversarial rounds, and an evaluation of five models against a comparison filter.

The released lines are dose statements, not complete directions: a conditional order prints as one fixed dose, a taper written as two orders prints as two daily doses, and weekly methotrexate coded as seven times a day prints as written; each needs a check (Limitations). <!-- paper/paper.md Introduction -->

## Objective

To determine whether a verified output layer guarantees that each drug name, dose, route, frequency, and daily total it fills from a slot equals the matching value of that patient's active medication order, and to measure, for five models, how much text the layer releases and what three open models write without it. The intended use is the medication section of a longer model output, such as a discharge summary or a patient portal reply; that use was not evaluated.

## Materials and Methods

**The layer:** The model writes plain text in which a slot of the form `{{field:id}}` stands for one field of the order with identifier `id`: the drug name, dose, route, frequency, daily total, or `sig` (dose, route, and frequency together). A value sentence consists of exactly the words and slots of one of four shapes for a single order, such as "Take {{drug}} {{dose}} {{route}} {{frequency}}", optionally followed by "which is {{daily}}". A framing sentence contains no slot and only words from a 67-word vocabulary for introducing a list and referring the patient to the care team. A deployment may add framing words; the proofs still hold, but the claims that framing sentences carry no medical content and that no drug name appears outside an instruction hold only for the default vocabulary. That the vocabulary contains no drug name is the author's judgment. <!-- DoseGate/Tokens.lean (vocab); DoseGate/Render.lean (shapes); eval/data/vocab_rxnorm_check.json; paper/paper.md Design -->

Model text may contain only American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) letters, digits, spaces, line breaks, and ten punctuation marks (Supplementary Section S4). No word may contain a digit or belong to one of five forbidden classes (353 words; Supplementary Section S4). For each slot the layer blocks if the order is missing, inactive, lacks the field, or shares a related drug name with another active order (Supplementary Section S13), and it rejects a repeated instruction or a minus or plus sign before a slot (Supplementary Section S1). By default it blocks an output that leaves an active order without an instruction (omittedOrder) or that has no active order. The daily slot multiplies the dose by the exact number of administrations per day and blocks for weekly, monthly, and as-needed orders. Insulin prints as "units," not "U," following Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) and Joint Commission designations;^20,21^ a clinical pharmacist must still check the ISMP entry for micrograms. Doses given as counts of tablets or puffs are blocked. <!-- DoseGate/Fhir.lean; DoseGate/Tokens.lean; DoseGate/Dec.lean; DoseGate/Units.lean; paper/paper.md Design -->

Insulin concentration changes the volume, not the unit: 20 units drawn on a U-100 syringe from a U-500 vial delivers 100 units, five times the printed dose, so a deployment must print the concentration from the dispensed product, which the released text does not state.^22^ <!-- paper/paper.md Design (Exact quantities); DailyMed Humulin R label, reference 22 -->

When an output is blocked, a fallback command prints one line per active order through the same proved code. Block reasons can quote the model and must never reach a patient. With the option `--allow-omissions`, which drops the check that every active order is named, an output of framing sentences alone can reach the patient with no medicine named, so a deployment using that option must block any output that names no order. <!-- paper/paper.md Design (Completeness and duplicates; Fallback) -->

**Specification, proofs, and trusted code:** The specification is a set of statements, written in Lean 4.35.0 with the core library only, about `render`, the function that takes the model's text and the orders and returns released text or a block reason (Table 1). The development contains 201 theorems in 2,082 lines of proof over 576 lines of definitions. The Lean kernel checks every theorem, and none uses an unproved assumption or evaluation by compiled code. Of the 201, 122 use only Lean's three standard axioms, 68 use some of them, and 11 use none. The principal theorems (Supplementary Table S1) establish fidelity: released text is the model's segments unchanged plus one formatted field per slot, from the single active order the slot names. They also cover value shapes, daily totals, completeness, model words, and signs. <!-- lean-toolchain; DoseGate/*.lean and DoseGate/Proofs/*.lean line counts; tests/Axioms.lean (201 theorems; 122, 68, and 11 tallied from tests/axioms_2026-11-07.txt, the saved output of lake env lean tests/Axioms.lean); paper/paper.md Specification and proofs -->

Before `render` runs, a reader converts the orders, which arrive as FHIR JavaScript Object Notation (JSON), into the Lean values the theorems are about. The Lean kernel does not check this reader. Its 739 non-blank lines of reader and command-line code are covered instead by 270 regression tests, each a fixed request with an expected reply, and by a fuzz test that mutates requests 20,000 times in 53 ways and checks that the reader's reply agrees with a separate Python program written to follow the same rules; the fuzz test shows that the reader follows its written rules, not that the rules are right. For an order it cannot fully interpret, the reader yields no dose, timing, or route, so by default every output for that patient is blocked (Supplementary Section S2). <!-- DoseGate/Json.lean 532 non-blank lines; Main.lean 207 non-blank lines; tests/run.sh (270 regression tests); tests/fuzz_reader.py (20,000 requests, 53 kinds); paper/paper.md Trusted code and Development -->

**Model evaluation:** Synthetic patients came from Synthea,^25^ 5,980 FHIR bundles. Three open models ran on one Apple M2 Max computer: Qwen3.5-4B^27^ at full precision with MLX LM 0.32.2^28^ and reasoning disabled, and MedGemma 4B^29^ and Llama 3.2 3B^30^ at 4-bit quantization through Ollama 0.40.14, so model and runtime vary together. ## Results

**Adversarial round:** Version 2 released 86 of the 138 round h2 requests (Table 2): 9 misstated an order, 4 made an unsupported claim, and 73 were labeled faithful, 19 of them giving one ingredient under two names. <!-- eval/data/labels_summary.json --> Of the other 54 faithful outputs, 19 omit at least one active order. The comparison filter released 128 of the 138 in a stored run (107 in the current build), 23 of them misstatements, including all 9 of the layer's. ## Discussion

A proved layer can bound what a model states about a dose, but four findings qualify the result. First, the usable-output rate varied with the model, from 98.3% to 1.7% after rewrites on the same cases, so a deployment must measure its model's block rate. The fallback prints one proved line per active order without any model (118 of 120 test cases), but it has no heading, daily total, reason for use, or warning. It also leaves out, silently, any order that is on hold, stopped, or marked doNotPerform, and it blocks when two active orders have related names. A deployment must add a heading and a care-team line from clinician-written text, withhold the fallback from as-needed and weekly orders unless a pharmacist has checked the lines, and check for one ingredient under two names first. <!-- eval/data/fallback_rate.json (eval/fallback_rate.py); paper/paper.md What the patient sees when output is blocked -->

In the slot conditions the model sees only each order's identifier and drug name, so its task is close to filling a template. Qwen3.5-4B released 118 of 120 test cases after rewrites in both slot conditions, matching the fallback; over the fallback, its text adds the two framing sentences from the prompt's example and, in 104 of 118 slot outputs, a daily total. Whether these help a patient was not measured; reading grades are in Supplementary Section S12. <!-- eval/data/final_gate_decisions_v2_qwen3.5-4b.json (slot and slot daily, released after rewrites); paper/paper.md What the model adds in this evaluation -->

Second, a proof establishes only the property it states: the theorems say what released text satisfies, not that any text is released, and they cannot show that the specification itself is right. Third, the models' errors without the layer arose where they had to compute: a daily total for a weekly order, a sum across drugs, or a doubled dose the prompt demanded. The design argument predicts that a number-comparing filter would handle these poorly, because a computed total is in no order and a harmful number can equal a legitimate one, as the doubled dose did in 47 attack outputs where it equaled the correct daily total (Supplementary Section S9); we did not measure such a filter. Fourth, the comparison filter released 56 misstatements from Llama 3.2 3B and, on round h2, 23 against the layer's 9; six of the nine lay in the shared reader and so tested the reader rather than the checks on model text (Table 3). <!-- eval/data/controls_v2_*.json; eval/data/labels_summary.json; paper/paper.md What a proof does and does not establish -->

We read the protection against numbers from an untrusted model as coming from narrowing the specification: each later version removed freedom the attacks had used, and version 2.1's released text is close to a template. This is the author's interpretation, not a separate test. Versions 2 and 2.1 also changed the input reader and the completeness default, which may account for part of the change.

Larger models answer medical questions more accurately^31^ and may follow the format more reliably, but that would change only the release rate. The hosted models released more often under a different sampling setting, so that comparison is not controlled. Constrained decoding^15^ could remove format failures, but a grammar cannot perform the layer's checks on substituted text, such as the printed decimal, the computed daily total, and related drug names; we did not test the pairing. <!-- eval/data/models_summary.json; eval/data/controls_v2_claude-*.json; paper/paper.md Model-agnostic, not model-independent -->

**Limitations:** The released instruction is not complete directions: it gives no tablet count, time of day, food or hold direction, indication, or warning, which a deployment must add from clinician-written text. Weekly, monthly, as-needed, conditional, and taper orders need a pharmacist check before any line reaches a patient. A conditional insulin order prints as one fixed dose, and a taper written as two orders prints as two daily doses; prednisone 20 mg and Deltasone 40 mg, both once a day, would give 60 mg a day if taken as a taper (Supplementary Section S10).

Chart errors print as written. Weekly methotrexate coded as seven times a day (Supplementary Section S10) is one that the layer cannot detect, so a deployment must check each order's timing against the labeled schedule. The related-name rule cannot see that a brand name, an abbreviation, or a combination product denotes an ingredient already ordered. Version 2.1 released 16 such pairs on round h2 by the author's unchecked reading, such as Lantus with insulin glargine, so a deployment with ingredient codes must check duplicate therapy first (Supplementary Section S6). A future version could block active orders that share an optional ingredient code, but no proof would cover the codes. Names such as "Cease Warfarin" also pass the drug-name rule (Supplementary Section S10). A superseded order is closed only by the exact reference `MedicationRequest/<id>`; with `--allow-omissions`, the other forms can print its old dose (Supplementary Section S15).

Fidelity to the order is not clinical safety. Label limits, interaction, renal and weight-based dosing, and duplicate therapy are outside the layer. The layer cannot check that a request holds every active order, so "Here is your medicine list." is untrue for a chart with an on-hold, stopped, or doNotPerform order. A deployment must make that check outside the layer, must not reuse the sentence unless a pharmacist has checked the chart, and must confirm that each order, including reflex and filler orders, is a prescriber's order.

The guarantee covers only the text the layer renders. Surrounding text may pass through only as framing sentences built from the 67 framing words or words the deployment adds. Those words are too few for a full discharge summary, so a deployment must keep doses, routes, frequencies, and drug names out of the rest of the text, which could otherwise state a different dose from the orders. <!-- DoseGate/Tokens.lean (vocab); paper/paper.md Intended use and scope -->

Free text on an active order blocks every output under the default rule, and no raw Synthea bundle prints (Supplementary Section S7). The option `--ignore-free-text` can print as-needed orders as scheduled doses and 455 normalized orders as "four times a day", so it must not be used on such records (Supplementary Section S15).

A bare "Take as needed." instruction blocks output. A "which is 100 mg a day" clause may read as an extra dose. SNOMED CT route codes are trusted and need clinician checking. The released line omits timing instructions, such as Humulin R about 30 minutes before meals or metformin with meals, which a deployment must add. Drug names with digits, such as 12 hr hydrocodone, need a pharmacist-approved display name (Supplementary Section S16).

The synthetic records were normalized, and their levothyroxine orders print 0.075 mg where US tablets are labeled in micrograms. The adversarial requests and labels came from one model family, with no human labeler and a Cohen's kappa of 0.50 on round h2. The adversarial rounds do not estimate a rate in any population. Qwen3.5-4B outputs were seen before version 2 was designed, the Claude runs used an unconfirmed sampling default, and no labeler read the hosted models' outputs. Version 2.1 has not been tested on a held-out round, and patient comprehension was not tested. ## Conclusion

A verified layer of 576 lines of Lean definitions, 2,082 lines of machine-checked proof, and 739 lines of tested reader and command-line code guarantees, by a proof about released text, that every drug name, dose, daily total, route, and frequency it fills from a slot equals the active order that slot names. The guarantee holds inside an instruction for that order, with every active order named under the default settings, whatever model writes the text. This is fidelity to the orders, not clinical appropriateness, and it rests on a tested reader and the author's judgment about the framing vocabulary. Version 2 released 9 misstatements in 86 outputs of an adversarial round written against it, mostly through the reader; version 2.1 blocks all nine on replay and is untested on a held-out round. Across five models, the lenient scoring script found no misstated dose, total, or frequency on the 120 test cases, and release rates after rewrites ranged from 98.3% for three models to 1.7% for Llama 3.2 3B. The intended use, the medication section of a longer model output, was not evaluated. Released lines are dose statements, not complete directions, so a deployment must add the checks listed under Limitations before patient use. <!-- paper/paper.md Conclusion and Results; eval/data/models_summary.json -->

**S2. Reader rules (tested, not proved):** An order is active only when its status is active, its intent is an order intent (order, original-order, reflex-order, filler-order, or instance-order), and `doNotPerform` is absent or false. A missing or non-R4 status or intent makes the whole request an error. Other valid statuses (on-hold, cancelled, completed, entered-in-error, stopped, draft, unknown) and other valid intents (proposal, plan, option) make the order inactive, and inactive orders are omitted without a block. An order keeps its drug name but yields no dose, timing, or route when it has a top-level key outside an allowlist of 37 keys; an extension at any depth; a security label; a `priorPrescription`, or another order's `priorPrescription` reference that is exactly `MedicationRequest/<id>` (the history form `MedicationRequest/<id>/_history/1` and the form `urn:uuid:<id>` do not close that order); a dispense validity period; `reportedBoolean` true or any `reportedReference` key; a non-continuous course of therapy; a dosage, timing, repeat, dose, or route key outside a short allowlist; more than one dosage instruction; a unit text that disagrees with its code, or a code system other than the Unified Code for Units of Measure (UCUM); a count dose (tablet, capsule, puff, drop); a route text that disagrees with its code; or, by default, a note or dosage free text. A dose with more than nine decimal places, a mantissa of 10^15 or more, or a zero amount yields no dose. Requests are rejected for duplicate keys, nesting deeper than 64 levels, a numeric literal longer than 1,000 characters or with an exponent of three or more digits, more than 20,000 numeric literals, more than 5,000 orders, an output longer than 65,536 characters, more than 400 slots, or more than two megabytes. **S13. Name rules and normalization:** A drug name prints only if it begins and ends with a letter, uses only ASCII letters, spaces, and hyphens, has at most 60 characters and five words, and contains no forbidden word, no word from the vocabulary or shape words, and none of 77 words that signal instructions, placeholder text, or non-drug content, such as "see", "call", "sliding", "scale", "milk", and "placeholder". The rule tests whole words only, so "Lisinopril 10 MG Oral Tablet" cannot print a second strength, but "Metformin fivemg" prints as written, and "Metformin treats diabetes" also prints. Two names are related if either has no word made of ASCII letters, either contains a character outside the ASCII range, they are equal ignoring case, they share a word, or one word begins the other and the shorter word has at least four characters. Normalization of the Synthea records, performed by the evaluation scripts and not by the layer, took every active order whose RxNorm display name gave exactly one strength in milligrams for a tablet or capsule, set the dose to that strength, the route to oral, and the drug name to the text before the strength, lowercased. **S14. Labeling protocol and addenda:** The protocol is in eval/labeling_protocol.md. The rubric counts as a misstatement a value that differs from the active orders, an inactive order presented as current, an instruction to skip, stop, double, or adjust a medicine, and, in round h2, an instruction attached to the wrong medicine or a conditional order presented as one fixed dose. The addendum dated November 5, 2026, adopted after labeling, sets three rules: (1) a text is faithful when each instruction it gives matches an active order, even when two active orders share a drug name or ingredient at different doses; (2) giving the same instruction twice for one order is a misstatement, because a patient can read it as a double dose; and (3) two identical active orders each given once are faithful. A clarification dated November 6, 2026 defines a repeated instruction as two or more clauses that each tell the patient to take the same order at its dose; extra words, such as a daily-total clause, do not change this. **S15. Behaviors checked with the built binary:** Superseded orders. With the exact reference `MedicationRequest/<id>`, the replacement closes the superseded order; with the history or `urn:uuid` form, the superseded order stays open. Under the default settings, every output for that patient is blocked (omittedOrder, missingField, or ambiguousDrug). The fallback is blocked too, with ambiguousDrug when the names are related and missingField otherwise. Under `--allow-omissions`, the exact form blocks outputs that name either order, while the history and `urn:uuid` forms print the superseded order's old dose ("Take lisinopril 10 mg by mouth twice a day.") when the names differ, and block with ambiguousDrug when they are related. An output with no slot is released in every form, so "Here is your medicine list." can reach the patient with neither order named. Doses given as counts are blocked with missingField. Held and stopped orders. An order with doNotPerform true, or with status on-hold or stopped, is left out of released text without a block. With an active metformin order and a warfarin order with doNotPerform true, the built binary prints "Here is your medicine list. Take metformin 500 mg by mouth twice a day." (paper/paper.md Limits). Reported orders. An order with `reportedBoolean` true, or with any `reportedReference` key, yields no dose, timing, or route, and under the default settings it blocks every output for that patient. Coded timing and free text. Under `--ignore-free-text`, the 455 normalized "Every four to six hours" orders with one milligram strength print as "four times a day" (376 at 1,300 mg a day and 79 at 1,600 mg a day), so the line states one frequency for an order whose record gives a range. None of the 193 raw "Every twelve hours as required" orders prints under the option, because each lacks a route and a usable dose; with usable structured fields, such an order prints as a scheduled dose. Weekly and as-needed lines. A weekly line does not say that the dose is not for daily use. A basal and mealtime insulin regimen, such as insulin glargine with insulin lispro, returns ambiguousDrug on both the model-text path and the fallback, so the patient gets no text by either path. An as-needed order whose as-needed reason is coded blocks the fallback. **S16. Other reader and printing limits:** The layer cannot print "swallow whole" (the word swallow is forbidden), so an extended-release product is released without that instruction. An order sent as a resource of another valid FHIR type, such as MedicationStatement, is skipped, and a deployment must send each order as a MedicationRequest. The layer cannot check that a request holds every active order the patient has.
