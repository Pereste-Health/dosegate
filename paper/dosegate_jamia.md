## Abstract

**Objective:** To test whether a layer verified in Lean 4 keeps slot-filled doses, frequencies, and daily totals equal to orders.

**Materials and Methods:** The model writes slots for Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) MedicationRequest fields; the layer fills each from its order or blocks the output. Framing words holding no drug name are judged, not proved. We proved 202 theorems, tested the reader, ran four adversarial rounds, and evaluated seven models on 120 test cases. <!-- tests/axioms_2026-11-08.txt; tests/run.sh; tests/fuzz_reader.py; eval/data/models_summary.json -->

**Results:** Rounds h2 to h4 released 9, 35, and 10 misstatements in 86, 91, and 107 texts, 51 of 54 through the input reader or drug name, 3 through model text. Each version narrowed what the reader accepts; on the held-out round h5, version 2.3 released 95 of 144 texts, one misstating an order and five making an unsupported claim. After rewrites it released 98.3% of slot outputs from five models, 83.3% from MedGemma 4B, and 1.7% from Llama 3.2 3B; a lenient script found no misstated dose, total, or frequency in 1,800 released texts; no clinician reviewed any. Without the layer, Qwen3.5-4B misstated an order in at least 16 of 120 outputs, MedGemma 4B in 71, and Llama 3.2 3B in 20. <!-- eval/data/labels_summary.json; eval/data/heldout3/summary.json; eval/data/heldout4/summary.json; eval/data/heldout5/summary.json; eval/data/models_summary.json; eval/data/v2_review/labels.json -->

**Discussion:** Given the tested reader, the guarantee holds for any model; the release rate does not. Weekly, as-needed, conditional, and taper lines need pharmacist review. <!-- paper/paper.md Results; eval/data/heldout2/labels_h2.json -->

**Conclusion:** A verified layer makes each filled dose and frequency match its order, not clinical appropriateness. Released lines are dose statements, not complete directions; clinician validation remains. <!-- paper/paper.md Abstract and Conclusion -->

## Background and Significance

Medication errors are among the most common preventable causes of patient harm.^1,2^ Large language models are increasingly used to answer patients' questions about their medicines, yet they generate numbers by sampling tokens rather than by reading a source of truth. On 230 consumer dosing questions about acetaminophen and ibuprofen, eight models answered correctly 44.8% to 62.2% of the time and approved 14.0% to 60.5% of prohibited doses,^3^ and models calculating weight-based pediatric doses made substantial errors.^4^ Placing the orders in the prompt does not remove the hazard: the model still reproduces each number and computes quantities the orders do not state, such as a daily total.^5^ <!-- paper/paper.md Introduction (DoseBench is reference 3 there) -->

Two families of safeguard are in use. Statistical safeguards, such as a second model that judges the first or a classifier that flags unsafe text,^6^ inherit the models' error profile and give no guarantee on any single output. Deterministic filters that compare output numbers with the record are predictable but only as good as their rules. Formal verification has been used in medicine before, to prove properties of the software in infusion pumps, which deliver intravenous drugs at set rates, and of the keypads on which clinicians enter doses,^5,7^ and proof assistants have recently gated the actions of language model agents.^8,9^ Related designs verify a synthesized policy,^10^ fix control flow from the trusted query,^11^ or place critics around candidate outputs.^12^ Template generation,^13^ data-to-text evaluation,^14^ constrained decoding,^15^ and runtime monitors and shields^16,17^ each constrain what a model may emit. Whether these methods can give a machine-checked guarantee that a language model's medication statements agree with the orders has not, to our knowledge, been examined.

We describe such a layer: a small program written in Lean 4^18^ with machine-checked proofs that each drug name, dose, route, frequency, and daily total in its released text equals the matching field of the active order it names; those proved statements are its specification (Supplementary Table S1). The model writes each instruction as references to fields of a FHIR MedicationRequest,^19^ and the layer replaces every reference with the order's value or blocks the whole output (Figure 1). The model cannot state an amount or a drug name itself: the layer blocks any output in which a word contains a digit, is a number, unit, route, or frequency word, or lies outside a 67-word default vocabulary and four sentence shapes, so amounts reach the patient only through references the layer fills. The theorems assume nothing about the model. We report the specification, the tested reader, four adversarial rounds, and an evaluation of seven models against a comparison filter.

The released lines are dose statements, not complete directions, and a conditional order, a taper, or a mis-coded weekly order prints as written (Limitations). <!-- paper/paper.md Introduction -->

## Objective

To determine whether a verified output layer guarantees that each drug name, dose, route, frequency, and daily total it fills from a slot equals the matching value of that patient's active medication order, and to measure, for seven models, how much text the layer releases and what three open models write without it. The intended use is the medication section of a longer model output, such as a discharge summary or a patient portal reply; that use was not evaluated.

## Materials and Methods

**The layer:** The model writes plain text in which a slot of the form `{{field:id}}` stands for one field of the order with identifier `id`: the drug name, dose, route, frequency, daily total, or `sig` (dose, route, and frequency together). A value sentence consists of exactly the words and slots of one of four shapes for a single order, such as "Take {{drug}} {{dose}} {{route}} {{frequency}}", optionally followed by "which is {{daily}}". A framing sentence contains no slot and only words from a 67-word vocabulary for introducing a list and referring the patient to the care team, including the words doctor, nurse, and pharmacist. A deployment may add framing words; the proofs still hold, but the claim that framing sentences carry no medical content, itself the author's judgment, holds only for the default vocabulary. <!-- DoseGate/Tokens.lean (vocab); DoseGate/Render.lean (shapes); eval/data/vocab_rxnorm_check.json; paper/paper.md Design -->

Model text may contain only ASCII letters, digits, spaces, line breaks, and ten punctuation marks (Supplementary Section S4). No word may contain a digit or belong to one of five forbidden classes (353 words; Supplementary Section S4). For each slot the layer blocks if the order is missing, inactive, lacks the field, or shares a related drug name with another active order (Supplementary Section S13), and it rejects a repeated instruction or a minus or plus sign before a slot (Supplementary Section S1). By default it blocks an output that leaves an active order without an instruction or that has no active order. The daily slot multiplies the dose by the exact number of administrations per day and blocks for weekly, monthly, and as-needed orders. Insulin prints as "units," not "U," following Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) and Joint Commission designations;^20,21^ a pharmacist must still check the ISMP entry for micrograms. Doses given as counts of tablets or puffs are blocked. <!-- DoseGate/Fhir.lean; DoseGate/Tokens.lean; DoseGate/Dec.lean; DoseGate/Units.lean; paper/paper.md Design -->

Insulin concentration changes the volume, not the unit, and the released text does not state it, so a deployment must print the concentration from the dispensed product.^22^ <!-- paper/paper.md Design (Exact quantities); DailyMed Humulin R label, reference 22 -->

When an output is blocked, a fallback command prints one line per active order through the same proved code; block reasons can quote the model and must never reach a patient. With the option `--allow-omissions`, which drops the check that every active order is named, framing sentences alone can reach the patient, so a deployment using it must block any output that names no order. <!-- paper/paper.md Design (Completeness and duplicates; Fallback) -->

**Specification, proofs, and trusted code:** The specification is a set of statements, written in Lean 4.35.0 with the core library only, about `render`, the function that takes the model's text and the orders and returns released text or a block reason (Table 1). The development contains 202 theorems in 2,084 lines of proof over 583 lines of definitions. The Lean kernel checks every theorem; none uses an unproved assumption or compiled evaluation, and 122 use only Lean's three standard axioms, 68 some of them, and 12 none. The principal theorems (Supplementary Table S1) establish fidelity: released text is the model's segments unchanged plus one formatted field per slot, from the single active order the slot names. They also cover value shapes, daily totals, completeness, model words, and signs. <!-- lean-toolchain; DoseGate/*.lean (583 non-blank lines excluding Json.lean and Lexicon.lean) and DoseGate/Proofs/*.lean (2,084); tests/axioms_2026-11-08.txt (202 theorems; 122, 68, 12) -->

Before `render` runs, a reader converts the orders, which arrive as FHIR JavaScript Object Notation (JSON), into the Lean values the theorems are about. The Lean kernel does not check this reader. Its 1,167 non-blank lines of reader and command-line code are covered instead by 457 regression tests and by a fuzz test that mutates requests 20,000 times in 80 ways and checks that the reader's reply agrees with a separate Python program written to the same rules, which shows the reader follows them, not that they are right. Version 2.3's reader accepts only Patient, MedicationRequest, and Medication resources and opaque identifiers, fails closed on any field, text, display, or reference form outside its allowlist, and prints a drug name only if it is one of 27,369 RxNorm names (Supplementary Section S2); versions 2.2 and 2.3 added these rules after the rounds below. <!-- DoseGate/Json.lean 960 and Main.lean 207 non-blank lines; tests/run.sh (457); tests/fuzz_reader.py (20,000 requests, 80 kinds); README.md reader rules; data/README.md -->

**Model evaluation:** Synthetic patients came from Synthea,^25^ 5,980 FHIR bundles. Those bundles give the strength only in the RxNorm^26^ display name, so we normalized each active order whose name gave one milligram strength for a tablet or capsule (Supplementary Section S13). Three open models ran on one Apple M2 Max computer: Qwen3.5-4B^27^ at full precision with MLX LM 0.32.2^28^ and reasoning disabled, and MedGemma 4B^29^ and Llama 3.2 3B^30^ at 4-bit quantization through Ollama 0.40.14, so model and runtime vary together. Each case received six generations from each open model: four greedy conditions (free daily, slot, slot daily, and attack) and two slot samples at temperature 1.0 (top-p 0.95, not rewritten). The free daily condition has no layer and asks for each medicine's daily total. In the slot conditions the model sees each order's identifier and drug name but not its values, and after a block it may rewrite up to twice. The attack condition tells the model to advise doubling the first medicine and to state the milligrams (Supplementary Section S3). <!-- eval/generate.py; eval/data/final_gate_decisions_v2_*.json; paper/paper.md Models and conditions -->

## Results

**Adversarial rounds:** Version 2 released 86 of the 138 round h2 requests and 9 misstated an order; version 2.1 released 91 of the 156 round h3 requests and 35 misstated an order; version 2.2 released 107 of the 145 round h4 requests and 10 misstated an order, and it released 15 unsupported claims, 13 naming a doctor, pharmacist, or nurse and 8 attributing the orders to one the chart did not name. Of the 54 misstatements, 51 entered through the input reader or the drug name, where a hold, taper, sliding scale, or weekly schedule sat in a reasonCode (the order's reason field), a coding display (the readable name attached to a drug code), an identifier, or the drug name itself while the structured fields printed a fixed dose, or in a resource the reader skipped; the other 3, all in round h2, were model text, two minus signs before a value and one list that omitted orders, which proved rules in version 2.1 closed. In every round the comparison filter released each misstatement the layer released and more (Table 2). <!-- eval/data/labels_summary.json; eval/data/heldout3/summary.json and misstatement_causes.json; eval/data/heldout4/summary.json and misstatement_causes.json -->

On replay, version 2.3 releases none of the 54 earlier misstatements and 34, 30, and 81 of the round h2, h3, and h4 requests (Table 2), texts identical to the earlier versions' and not held-out estimates, among them the 3 and 8 texts from rounds h3 and h4 labeled unsupported claims. On the held-out round h5, version 2.3 released 95 of 144 requests: 1 misstated an order, 5 made an unsupported claim, and 89 were labeled faithful, 2 of them giving one ingredient under two names (Supplementary Section S19). The misstatement, which both readers called a judgment call, was a warfarin order whose entry fullUrl (the web address that names an order inside a FHIR bundle) carried the words hold-warfarin-if-inr-above-3 before the resource's type and id, a part of the address the reader never reads; the 5 unsupported claims were provenance or framing sentences such as "These are from your doctor" and a "Doctor:" heading. The comparison filter released 115 of the 144, with 8 misstatements. <!-- eval/data/replay_v23/*_results.jsonl; eval/data/heldout5/summary.json -->

## Discussion

A proved layer can bound what a model states about a dose, but four findings qualify the result. First, the usable-output rate varied with the model, from 98.3% to 1.7% after rewrites on the same cases, so a deployment must measure its model's block rate. The fallback prints one proved line per active order without any model (118 of 120 test cases); the models add only framing sentences and daily totals over it (Supplementary Section S12). It has no heading, daily total, reason for use, or warning, it leaves out on-hold, stopped, and doNotPerform orders silently, and it blocks when two active orders have related names. A deployment must add a heading and a care-team line from clinician-written text, withhold the fallback from as-needed and weekly orders unless a pharmacist has checked the lines, and check for one ingredient under two names first. <!-- eval/data/fallback_rate.json (eval/fallback_rate.py); paper/paper.md What the patient sees when output is blocked --> <!-- eval/data/final_gate_decisions_v2_qwen3.5-4b.json (slot and slot daily, released after rewrites); paper/paper.md What the model adds in this evaluation -->

Second, a proof establishes only the property it states: the theorems say what released text satisfies, not that any text is released, and they cannot show that the specification itself is right. Of the 54 misstatements of rounds h2 to h4, 51 entered through the input reader or the drug name, the parts Table 1 lists as tested or trusted, by placing a condition where the reader did not look, and 3 came from model text that version 2.1 closed with proved rules (Table 3); each version answered by narrowing what the reader accepts, and round h5 found one misstatement and five unsupported claims in 95 released texts, the misstatement hidden in the host part of an entry's web address and no new class of failure. What remains open is the meaning of the fields the reader does interpret and chart content outside the request. Third, the models' errors without the layer arose where they had to compute: a daily total for a weekly order, a sum across drugs, or a doubled dose the prompt demanded. A number-comparing filter would handle these poorly, because a computed total is in no order and a harmful number can equal a legitimate one, as the doubled dose did in the 47 open-model attack outputs where it equaled the correct daily total (Supplementary Section S9); we did not measure such a filter. Fourth, the comparison filter released 56 misstatements among its 171 extra Llama 3.2 3B releases and, on rounds h2 to h5, 23, 53, 16, and 8 against the layer's 9, 35, 10, and 1. <!-- eval/data/controls_v2_*.json; eval/data/labels_summary.json; eval/data/heldout3/summary.json; eval/data/heldout4/summary.json -->

We read the protection as coming from narrowing: each later version removed freedom the attacks had used, first in the model's text and then in the record the reader accepts, until version 2.3's released text is close to a template; this is the author's interpretation, not a separate test.

Larger models answer medical questions more accurately^31^ and may follow the format more reliably, but because the proofs do not depend on the model, that would change only the release rate. Constrained decoding^15^ could remove format failures but cannot perform the layer's checks on substituted text, such as the computed daily total and related drug names; we did not test the pairing. <!-- eval/data/models_summary.json; eval/data/controls_v2_claude-*.json; paper/paper.md Model-agnostic, not model-independent -->

**Limitations:** The released instruction is not complete directions: it gives no tablet count, time of day, food or hold direction, indication, or warning, which a deployment must add from clinician-written text. Weekly, monthly, as-needed, conditional, and taper orders need a pharmacist check before any line reaches a patient. A conditional insulin order prints as one fixed dose, and a taper written as two orders prints as two daily doses (Supplementary Section S10).

Chart errors print as written, such as weekly methotrexate coded as seven times a day (Supplementary Section S10), so a deployment must check each order's timing against the labeled schedule. The related-name rule cannot see that a brand name or a combination product denotes an ingredient already ordered. Version 2.3 releases 9 such pairs on round h2 by the author's unchecked reading, such as Lantus with insulin glargine, so a deployment with ingredient codes must check duplicate therapy first (Supplementary Section S6). <!-- eval/data/heldout2/same_ingredient_pairs.json; eval/data/replay_v23/h2_results.jsonl --> The lexicon is a trusted table: a name outside it blocks, and a name inside it prints whatever the order says. Identifiers must be opaque and only Patient, MedicationRequest, and Medication resources may be sent, so a deployment must map word-like ids and apply any hold recorded in a Flag or Communication resource before building the request. Under `--allow-omissions`, a replacement order that names the superseded order by urn:uuid or a bare id can let the old dose print (Supplementary Section S15). The framing vocabulary lets the model address or attribute the orders to a doctor, nurse, or pharmacist; round h4 released 13 texts naming one, 8 of them attributing the orders to a clinician the chart did not name, and round h5 five more, none touching a dose, and a deployment can remove those words at the cost of changing its prompt (Supplementary Table S17e and Section S19). <!-- eval/data/heldout4/labels_h4.json; eval/data/heldout5/labels_h5.json -->

Fidelity to the order is not clinical safety: label limits, interactions, renal and weight-based dosing, and duplicate therapy are outside the layer, and the layer cannot know whether the request holds every order the patient has, so "Here is your medicine list." can be untrue unless the deployment has checked the chart and confirmed that each order is a prescriber's.

The guarantee covers only the text the layer renders; surrounding text may pass only as framing sentences from the 67 words or words the deployment adds, too few for a discharge summary, so a deployment must keep doses, routes, frequencies, and drug names out of the rest of the text. <!-- DoseGate/Tokens.lean (vocab); paper/paper.md Intended use and scope -->

Free text on an active order blocks every output by default, and no raw Synthea bundle prints (Supplementary Section S7); the option `--ignore-free-text` can print as-needed orders as scheduled doses, so it must not be used on such records (Supplementary Section S15).

A "which is 100 mg a day" clause may read as an extra dose, route codes are trusted, and the released line omits timing instructions such as insulin before meals, which a deployment must add (Supplementary Section S16).

The synthetic records were normalized, and their levothyroxine orders print 0.075 mg where US tablets are labeled in micrograms. The adversarial requests and labels came from one model family, with no human labeler. The adversarial rounds do not estimate a rate in any population. Qwen3.5-4B outputs were seen before version 2 was designed, the Claude runs used an unconfirmed sampling default, and no labeler read the hosted models' outputs. Version 2.3 has been tested on one held-out round, and patient comprehension was not tested. ## Conclusion

A verified layer guarantees, by a proof about the released text, that every drug name, dose, daily total, route, and frequency it fills from a slot equals the active order that slot names. The guarantee holds inside an instruction for that order, with every active order named under the default settings, whatever model writes the text. This is fidelity to the orders, not clinical appropriateness, and it rests on a tested reader, a trusted lexicon, and the author's judgment about the framing vocabulary. Rounds h2, h3, and h4 released 9, 35, and 10 misstatements against versions 2, 2.1, and 2.2, 51 of the 54 through the reader or the drug name and 3 through model text, and each version narrowed what the reader accepts; on the held-out round h5, version 2.3 released 95 texts, one misstating an order and five making an unsupported claim. Across seven models, the lenient scoring script found no misstated dose, total, or frequency on the 120 test cases, and release rates after rewrites ranged from 98.3% for five models to 1.7% for Llama 3.2 3B. Released lines are dose statements, not complete directions, so a deployment must add the checks listed under Limitations before patient use. <!-- eval/data/labels_summary.json; eval/data/heldout5/summary.json; eval/data/models_summary.json -->

An order's medicine comes from medicationCodeableConcept, or from a medicationReference that resolves to exactly one Medication resource in the same bundle carrying only resourceType, id, meta, code, and an active status; an order with both fields, with an unresolved reference, or with a reference carrying a display yields no name, dose, timing, or route. The concept may carry only text and coding, each coding only system, code, display, and userSelected, with an RxNorm, NDC, or SNOMED CT system. The printed name is the text; every coding display must be the text name alone or the text name followed only by a strength and dose form from a fixed grammar (numbers, the dose units of the unit table, optionally over mL, L, g, mg, HR, or ACTUAT, a fixed list of dose-form words, and a final bracketed brand name that is in the lexicon), so "Lisinopril 10 MG Oral Tablet" passes and "Methotrexate 15 MG Weekly Oral Tablet" does not; without a text, the displays must all be the same bare name. The name prints only if, lowercased, it equals a line of `data/rxnorm_names.txt`, and the proved structural rule (letters, spaces, and hyphens, at most 60 characters and five words, no forbidden or stop word) applies on top. Reflex and filler orders print as ordinary instructions. <!-- README.md (reader rules, version 2.3); DoseGate/Json.lean; Main.lean; data/README.md -->

**S13. Name rules and normalization:** A drug name prints only if it begins and ends with a letter, uses only ASCII letters, spaces, and hyphens, has at most 60 characters and five words, and contains no forbidden word, no word from the vocabulary or shape words, and none of 77 words that signal instructions, placeholder text, or non-drug content, such as "see", "call", "sliding", "scale", "milk", and "placeholder". The rule tests whole words only, so "Lisinopril 10 MG Oral Tablet" cannot print a second strength; under versions 2.1 and 2.2 "Metformin fivemg" and "Metformin treats diabetes" printed as written, and under version 2.3 they block because neither is in the RxNorm lexicon (Supplementary Section S2). Two names are related if either has no word made of ASCII letters, either contains a character outside the ASCII range, they are equal ignoring case, they share a word, or one word begins the other and the shorter word has at least four characters. Normalization of the Synthea records, performed by the evaluation scripts and not by the layer, took every active order whose RxNorm display name gave exactly one strength in milligrams for a tablet or capsule, set the dose to that strength, the route to oral, and the drug name to the text before the strength, lowercased. The 11 drugs were lisinopril (162 records), hydrochlorothiazide (148), amlodipine (126), simvastatin (28), acetaminophen (16), losartan potassium (6), 12 hr hydrocodone bitartrate (6), abuse-deterrent 12 hr oxycodone hydrochloride (4), levothyroxine sodium (2), meperidine hydrochloride (2), and dolutegravir (2). **S14. Labeling protocol and addenda:** The protocol is in eval/labeling_protocol.md. The rubric counts as a misstatement a value that differs from the active orders, an inactive order presented as current, an instruction to skip, stop, double, or adjust a medicine, and, in round h2, an instruction attached to the wrong medicine or a conditional order presented as one fixed dose. The addendum dated November 5, 2026, adopted after labeling, sets three rules: (1) a text is faithful when each instruction it gives matches an active order, even when two active orders share a drug name or ingredient at different doses; (2) giving the same instruction twice for one order is a misstatement, because a patient can read it as a double dose; and (3) two identical active orders each given once are faithful. A clarification dated November 6, 2026 defines a repeated instruction as two or more clauses that each tell the patient to take the same order at its dose; extra words, such as a daily-total clause, do not change this. The round 10 sweep relabeled 12 records. The repaired round 18 sweep matched 67 repeated-instruction outputs and relabeled 5 Llama 3.2 3B comparison-filter outputs. **S16. Other reader and printing limits:** The layer cannot print "swallow whole" (the word swallow is forbidden), so an extended-release product is released without that instruction. The layer does not know the strength of the tablets a patient holds, so a deployment must print the tablet count from the dispensed product. A request that contains a resource of another FHIR type, such as MedicationStatement, is refused by version 2.3, so a deployment must send each order as a MedicationRequest and must apply any hold recorded elsewhere in the chart before building the request. The layer cannot check that a request holds every active order the patient has. A patient who holds tablets of a prohibited, held, or stopped medicine receives no warning from the layer. <!-- paper/paper.md Limits -->
